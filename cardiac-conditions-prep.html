1 · Heart failure about 8 min

Heart failure means the heart can't pump enough to meet the body's needs. Where the blood backs up depends on which side fails.

Left-sided failureRight-sided failure
Backs up intoThe lungsThe body's veins
Key findingsDyspnea, orthopnea, waking up breathless at night (PND), crackles, cough with pink frothy sputum, S3, low SpO₂JVD, dependent pitting edema, enlarged liver, ascites, weight gain, poor appetite
Common causesMI, long-standing hypertension, cardiomyopathy, valve diseaseLeft-sided failure (most common), COPD and pulmonary hypertension (cor pulmonale)

Reduced vs. preserved ejection fraction. HFrEF (EF 40% or less) is a weak ventricle that can't squeeze. HFpEF (EF 50% or more) is a stiff ventricle that can't relax and fill, often from hypertension and aging.

Teaching that prevents readmission: weigh daily at the same time on the same scale, and report a gain of 2–3 lb (about 1 kg) in a day or 5 lb (about 2 kg) in a week. Limit sodium as ordered. Report new swelling, a cough or needing more pillows to sleep.

2 · Shock about 8 min

Shock is poor tissue perfusion. Early on, BP may look normal while the body compensates with a fast heart rate, fast breathing, low urine output and restlessness. Treatment aims for a MAP of at least 65 mm Hg. The type tells you what to do, and the fastest clue is usually volume status (neck veins and lungs) and skin.

Worked example · read it step by step

Mr. Samuel Ortiz, 67, is 2 days past a large anterior MI. He is restless. BP 84/56, HR 118, RR 26, SpO₂ 89%. Skin is cool and clammy. JVD is present at 45°, crackles are heard halfway up both lungs, and there is a new S3. Urine output was 15 mL in the last hour.

  1. Is this shock? Yes: hypotension plus signs of poor perfusion (restlessness, cool skin, urine output under 30 mL/hr).
  2. Check the tank. JVD and crackles mean preload is high. The problem isn't missing volume, so this isn't hypovolemic shock.
  3. Check the skin. Cool and clammy means the vessels are clamped down. Early septic or anaphylactic shock would usually be warm and flushed.
  4. Look for the cause. A large anterior MI damaged the pumping muscle. A full tank with a failing pump is cardiogenic shock.
  5. Decide the priority. Call for help, give oxygen, and anticipate an inotrope such as dobutamine. Lower the heart's workload and measure urine output hourly. Question any large fluid bolus, because it would worsen the pulmonary edema.

Valve disease

A rule that sorts murmurs: stenosis is heard when a valve should be open, and regurgitation when it should be closed. The aortic valve is open in systole and the mitral valve is open in diastole.

Valve problemMurmurOther findings
Aortic stenosisSystolic, crescendo-decrescendo, 2nd ICS right sternal border, radiates to the carotidsClassic triad: angina, syncope, dyspnea or heart failure. Use caution with nitrates
Aortic regurgitationDiastolic, blowing, left sternal borderWidened pulse pressure, bounding pulses

Mitral regurgitationHolosystolic, blowing, at the apex, radiates to the left axillaFatigue, dyspnea, S3; often after MI or mitral valve prolapse
