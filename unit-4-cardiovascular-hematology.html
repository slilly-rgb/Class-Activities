Cardiovascular and hematology

The heart pumps, the vessels carry, and the blood delivers oxygen and seals leaks. This unit follows that system from normal structure and electricity through the conditions you will see on every unit: heart failure, dysrhythmias, heart attacks, vascular disease, shock, and blood disorders.

The unit has nine sections. Each one works like the pathways you've used before: an introduction, terminology, foundations and key concepts, condition pages with quick checks, a section test, and a list of what to bring to class. The foundations sections (1, 3, and 8) come before the conditions that build on them.

Section 1 · Cardiac Foundations (Mechanical)

The pump behind every finding

Before you can recognize heart failure, a valve problem, or shock, you need a clear picture of the normal pump: how blood moves through it, what sets how much it ejects, and what your assessment and lab values are telling you.

This section is the mechanical half of cardiac foundations. Section 3 covers the electrical half. Work through both before the condition sections, because every condition in this unit is one of these normal processes breaking down.

Keep three questions in mind: How does blood get through the heart? What decides how much the heart pumps? What do the assessment, labs, and tests tell you about the pump?

Learning objectives

When you finish, you should be able to:

  1. Define the medical terms used to describe cardiac structure and function.
  2. Trace blood flow through the chambers, valves, and great vessels, and name what each coronary artery supplies.
  3. Explain the cardiac cycle and how heart rate, preload, afterload, and contractility determine cardiac output.
  4. Apply core principles (Frank-Starling law, afterload, oxygen supply and demand, coronary filling in diastole, MAP, compensation, filling time) to cardiac findings.
  5. Identify auscultation sites, normal and extra heart sounds, and peripheral cardiovascular findings.
  6. Interpret cardiac labs, including the troponin trend, BNP, potassium, magnesium, lipids, and coagulation studies.
  7. Describe the nursing care before and after common cardiac diagnostic tests.

Section 1 · Cardiac Foundations (Mechanical)

Heart anatomy and blood flow

Two pumps share one muscle. The right side receives oxygen-poor blood and pushes it through the low-pressure lungs. The left side receives oxygen-rich blood and pushes it to the whole body at high pressure, which is why its wall is so much thicker.

Layers of the heart

LayerWhat it isWhen it goes wrong
PericardiumTwo-layer sac around the heart with a small amount of lubricating fluidPericarditis (inflamed sac); pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade (fluid in the sac)
EpicardiumOuter surface of the heart wall; the coronary arteries run hereCoronary artery disease
MyocardiumThick middle layer of cardiac muscle that contractsMyocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, myocarditis
EndocardiumSmooth inner lining of the chambers and valvesInfective endocarditis

Blood flow through the heart

Vena cavae → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonic valve → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → body

The atrioventricular (AV) valves (tricuspid and mitral) sit between atria and ventricles. The chordae tendineae and papillary muscles anchor them so they don't flip backward when the ventricles squeeze. The semilunar valves (pulmonic and aortic) sit at the exits of the ventricles.

Coronary arteries

The heart feeds itself through the coronary arteries, which branch off the aorta just above the aortic valve.

ArterySuppliesWhy the nurse cares
Right coronary artery (RCA)Right atrium and ventricle, inferior wall of the left ventricle, and the SA and AV nodes in most peopleBlockage can cause bradycardia, heart block, and right ventricular failure
Left anterior descending (LAD)Anterior wall of the left ventricle and most of the septumSupplies the largest share of pumping muscle; blockage is high risk

Section 1 · Cardiac Foundations (Mechanical)

The cardiac cycle and cardiac output

Each heartbeat has a filling phase and an emptying phase. How much blood leaves with each beat, and how often the heart beats, determine how much reaches the body every minute.

Systole and diastole

Diastole

Relax and fill

  • Ventricles relax; AV valves open; ventricles fill
  • Atrial contraction at the end of diastole tops them off (the "atrial kick," about 20–30% of filling)
  • Coronary arteries fill mainly now
  • Takes about two thirds of each beat at a normal rate
Systole

Squeeze and eject

  • Ventricles contract; AV valves close (S1)
  • Semilunar valves open; blood is ejected
  • Ventricles relax; semilunar valves close (S2)
  • Systolic BP reflects this phase

Cardiac output and its levers

Cardiac output (CO) = Heart rate (HR) × Stroke volume (SV)
Normal CO ≈ 4–8 L/min · Ejection fraction (EF) = SV ÷ end-diastolic volume, normal about 50–70%

LeverWhat it meansPicture itRaised by
PreloadHow much the ventricle is stretched by the blood in it at the end of fillingA rubber band pulled back further snaps harder, up to a pointFluid overload, IV fluids, heart failure
AfterloadThe resistance the ventricle pushes against to eject bloodPushing open a heavy doorHypertension, vasoconstriction, aortic stenosis
ContractilityHow forcefully the muscle squeezes, apart from stretchA strong hand vs. a tired hand squeezing the same ballSympathetic stimulation, inotropes (dobutamine, digoxin)
Heart rateBeats per minuteRefilling a bucket too fast leaves it half fullFever, pain, hypovolemia, anxiety

Cardiac output calculator

Try HR 150, EDV 110, ESV 75

Mean arterial pressure

MAP is the average pressure pushing blood into the organs: MAP = (SBP + 2 × DBP) ÷ 3. Diastole counts twice because it lasts about twice as long. A MAP of at least 65 mm Hg is generally needed to perfuse the brain, heart, and kidneys.

Section 1 · Cardiac Foundations (Mechanical)

Key concepts

These principles explain why the findings in this unit happen. Learn them once and you can reason through conditions you haven't memorized.

C1

Frank-Starling law

The more the ventricle is stretched by filling, the harder it contracts, up to a point. Past that point, more stretch makes it weaker.

↑ preload → ↑ stroke volume … until overstretched

Picture it: A rubber band snaps back harder the further you pull it, until it is stretched out and goes slack.

Where you'll see it
  • Giving fluids raises stroke volume in a dehydrated client
  • In heart failure, extra volume no longer helps and backs up into the lungs
  • Why diuretics help an overstretched heart
C2

Afterload sets the workload

The harder it is to push blood out, the more work and oxygen each beat costs, and the less blood is ejected.

↑ afterload (high BP, narrow valve) → ↑ workload, ↓ stroke volume

Picture it: Pushing open a heavy door takes more effort than a light one, and you get through more slowly.

Where you'll see it
  • Hypertension thickens the left ventricle over time
  • Aortic stenosis
  • Vasodilators and ACE inhibitors lower afterload in heart failure
C3

Oxygen supply vs. demand

Heart muscle is injured when its oxygen demand outruns the oxygen delivered through the coronary arteries.

Demand: HR, contractility, afterload · Supply: coronary flow, O₂ content

Picture it: A delivery team that keeps up on a slow day falls behind when orders double and the road narrows.

Where you'll see it
  • Angina with exertion
  • Tachycardia is dangerous in coronary disease
  • Rest, oxygen if low, and beta blockers reduce demand
C4

The heart feeds itself in diastole

The coronary arteries fill mainly during diastole, when the heart muscle relaxes and stops squeezing its own vessels.

↑ heart rate → shorter diastole → less coronary filling

Picture it: You can only refill a water bottle when the cap is off; a squeezed bottle won't fill.

Where you'll see it
  • Fast heart rates can cause ischemia
  • Low diastolic BP lowers coronary perfusion
  • Why rate control matters after MI
C5

Perfusion needs pressure

Organs need enough average pressure to push blood through them. A MAP of at least 65 mm Hg is the usual minimum.

MAP = (SBP + 2 × DBP) ÷ 3

Picture it: A sprinkler needs enough water pressure to reach the far end of the lawn.

Where you'll see it
  • Shock and hypotension
  • Low urine output and confusion signal poor perfusion
  • Vasopressors are titrated to a MAP goal
C6

Compensation protects, then harms

When output falls, baroreceptors trigger the sympathetic nervous system and the RAAS to raise heart rate, constrict vessels, and hold fluid. This supports BP short term but overworks the heart long term.

↓ BP → ↑ HR, vasoconstriction, Na⁺ and water retention

Picture it: Borrowing on a credit card keeps you afloat this month and buries you later.

Where you'll see it
  • Early shock: BP looks normal while HR rises
  • Heart failure worsens as the heart is overworked
  • Why ACE inhibitors and beta blockers help heart failure
C7

Heart rate trades filling time for speed

Faster rates raise cardiac output only to a point. At very fast rates diastole is so short that the ventricles can't fill, so stroke volume and output fall.

CO = HR × SV; at very high HR, SV drops sharply

Picture it: Refilling a bucket too quickly leaves it half full each trip.

Where you'll see it
  • Rapid atrial fibrillation and SVT lower BP
  • Tachycardia in shock is compensation, until it isn't
  • Why rate control helps in atrial fibrillation

Section 1 · Cardiac Foundations (Mechanical)

Cardiac assessment

Your stethoscope and your hands tell you how well the pump is working long before a lab result comes back.

Where to listen

S1 ("lub"): the mitral and tricuspid valves closing at the start of systole. Loudest at the apex.

S2 ("dub"): the aortic and pulmonic valves closing at the start of diastole. Loudest at the base.

S3: just after S2, as blood rushes into a full or failing ventricle. In adults it usually means fluid overload or heart failure.

S4: just before S1, as the atria push blood into a stiff ventricle (hypertension, ischemia, hypertrophy).

Murmur: a swishing sound from turbulent flow through a narrowed or leaking valve or an abnormal opening. Friction rub: a scratchy, high-pitched sound from an inflamed pericardium.

Health history clues

Ask about chest pain (onset, location, radiation, quality, what helps), shortness of breath with activity or lying flat, waking up breathless at night, how many pillows they sleep on, swelling, fatigue, palpitations, dizziness or fainting, and weight gain. Atypical symptoms (fatigue, nausea, jaw or back discomfort) are common in women, older adults, and people with diabetes.

Lab values tell you whether heart muscle is being injured, whether the heart is stretched by too much fluid, and whether the blood chemistry is setting the stage for a dangerous rhythm.

Worked example · read it step by step

Mr. Leon Price, 58, came to the ED with "indigestion" and unusual fatigue since yesterday. He vomited three times overnight. High-sensitivity troponin I: 12 ng/L at 0 hours, 96 at 3 hours, 284 at 6 hours (normal ≤ 20). Potassium 3.3 mEq/L. Magnesium 1.9 mEq/L.

  1. Compare each value to the range. The first troponin is normal, but it was drawn early. A normal early troponin does not rule out MI.
  2. Read the trend, not one value. A rising troponin across serial draws means heart muscle is being injured right now.
  3. Connect it to the person. "Indigestion" and fatigue are atypical MI symptoms, so don't wait for classic chest pain.
  4. Look for what makes it more dangerous. Potassium of 3.3 after vomiting raises the risk of ventricular dysrhythmias in ischemic muscle.
  5. Decide the priority. Report the troponin trend and the potassium now, keep him on continuous cardiac monitoring and rest, and anticipate potassium replacement by infusion pump or by mouth, never IV push.

    Section 2 · Cardiac Conditions (Mechanical)

    When the pump breaks down

    Most mechanical heart problems come down to two things: blood backs up behind the failing part, and too little blood moves forward. Where it backs up tells you which side or structure is failing.

    The first half covers adult conditions: heart failure, inflammatory and infectious conditions, valve disease, and cardiomyopathies. The second half covers infants and children: fetal circulation, heart failure in children, and congenital heart defects. Section 1's anatomy and cardiac output are the foundation for both.

    For every condition ask: Where does blood back up? What happens to forward flow? What reduces the heart's workload?

    Learning objectives

    When you finish, you should be able to:

    1. Define the terms used to describe adult and pediatric mechanical heart conditions.
    2. Apply core principles (backward and forward failure, compensation, stiffness, pressure gradients, volume overload, obstruction, birth changes, mixing, the ductus, infant demand) to cardiac findings.
    3. Differentiate left- from right-sided heart failure and HFrEF from HFpEF, and describe heart failure medications and teaching.
    4. Recognize pericarditis, cardiac tamponade, infective endocarditis, and myocarditis, and the nursing priorities for each.
    5. Distinguish stenosis from regurgitation and describe the major valve disorders and cardiomyopathies.
    6. Trace fetal circulation and explain the changes at birth.
    7. Classify congenital heart defects, and describe the findings and nursing care for PDA, ASD, VSD, coarctation, tetralogy of Fallot, and transposition.

    Key concepts

    These principles explain why each condition causes the signs it does. The first three apply to adult hearts; the rest explain congenital defects and heart failure in children.

    Adult conditions

    C1

    Backward and forward failure

    When a chamber fails, blood backs up behind it (congestion) and too little moves ahead of it (low output).

    Left fails → lungs congest + low systemic output · Right fails → veins congest

    Picture it: A stalled car backs up traffic behind it and leaves an empty road ahead.

    Where you'll see it
    • Left- vs. right-sided heart failure
    • Mitral stenosis backing blood into the lungs
    • Cardiogenic shock
    C2

    Compensation becomes the problem

    When output falls, the sympathetic system and RAAS raise heart rate, constrict vessels, and retain sodium and water. This helps short term but overworks and remodels the heart long term.

    ↓ CO → ↑ SNS + RAAS → ↑ HR, afterload, fluid → more strain

    Picture it: Revving a struggling engine harder gets you up the hill but wears it out.

    Where you'll see it
    • Why ACE inhibitors, ARBs, beta blockers, and spironolactone help heart failure
    • Fluid retention and weight gain
    • Tachycardia in failing hearts
    C3

    A stiff chamber fills poorly

    A ventricle that can't relax can't fill, even at normal pressure. Output falls and pressure backs up, even with a normal ejection fraction.

    ↓ compliance → ↓ filling → ↑ filling pressure

    Picture it: Filling a thick rubber balloon takes far more pressure than a thin one.

    Where you'll see it
    • HFpEF
    • Hypertrophic and restrictive cardiomyopathy
    • Cardiac tamponade (external squeeze)

    Congenital and pediatric conditions

    C4

    Pressure gradients drive shunts

    Blood flows from higher pressure to lower pressure. After birth the left side of the heart has higher pressure than the right, so an opening between them sends blood left to right.

    Flow direction: high pressure → low pressure · Left side > right side after birth

    Picture it: Open a gate between a full pool and an empty one and water rushes toward the empty side.

    Where you'll see it
    • ASD, VSD, and PDA all shunt left to right after birth
    • The bigger the opening and the pressure difference, the bigger the shunt
    • Before birth, right-sided pressure is higher, so fetal shunts go right to left
    C5

    Resistance changes at birth

    At birth, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) falls and systemic vascular resistance (SVR) rises. PVR keeps falling for 6 to 8 weeks, so left-to-right shunts grow during that time.

    First breath: ↓ PVR · Cord clamped: ↑ SVR

    Picture it: Opening a second door in a crowded room: more people go through the easier door the wider it opens.

    Where you'll see it
    • A large VSD often causes heart failure at 4 to 8 weeks, not at birth
    • Knee-chest position and squatting raise SVR and push more blood to the lungs in tetralogy of Fallot
    • Eisenmenger syndrome: damaged lungs raise PVR until the shunt reverses
    C6

    Cyanosis means blue blood reached the body

    A child is cyanotic only when unoxygenated blood bypasses the lungs and reaches the body, through a right-to-left shunt or mixing. Cyanosis becomes visible when about 5 g/dL of hemoglobin is unoxygenated.

    Right-to-left shunt or mixing → ↓ SpO₂ → cyanosis

    Picture it: Pour some dirty water into a clean water line and the faucet runs cloudy, no matter how clean the main supply is.

    Where you'll see it
    • Tetralogy of Fallot: blue blood crosses the VSD into the aorta
    • Transposition: blue blood circulates straight back to the body
    • Chronic hypoxemia causes polycythemia and clubbing; anemia can hide cyanosis
    C7

    The ductus answers to oxygen and prostaglandins

    High oxygen levels and falling prostaglandins make the ductus arteriosus constrict after birth. Prostaglandin E1 keeps it open; prostaglandin inhibitors (indomethacin, ibuprofen) help close it.

    ↑ O₂, ↓ prostaglandins → closes · PGE1 → stays open

    Picture it: A door with a spring closer: oxygen lets it swing shut, and prostaglandin is the doorstop holding it open.

    Where you'll see it
    • PDA in premature infants: indomethacin or ibuprofen to close it
    • Ductal-dependent defects (TGA, severe coarctation): PGE1 to keep it open
    • Watch for apnea with PGE1
    C8

    Volume overload strains the heart

    Extra volume stretches heart chambers. Up to a point the heart contracts harder (Frank-Starling law). Past that point it dilates, weakens, and fails, and fluid backs up into the lungs and liver.

    ↑ volume → ↑ stretch → ↑ force … until failure

    Picture it: A rubber band snaps back harder the more you stretch it, until it's stretched too far and goes slack.

    Where you'll see it
    • Left-to-right shunts flood the lungs and left heart
    • Heart failure in infants: tachypnea, hepatomegaly, poor feeding
    • Why diuretics and fluid monitoring help
    C9

    Infants have high demand and little reserve

    Infants use more oxygen and calories per kilogram than adults, and raise cardiac output mainly by raising heart rate. Feeding is an infant's exercise, so cardiac problems show up during feeds and as poor growth.

    Cardiac output = heart rate × stroke volume (small, fixed stroke volume in infants)

    Picture it: A small car engine already running at high RPM has little room to go faster up a hill.

    Where you'll see it
    • Sweating, tachypnea, and stopping to rest during feeds
    • Failure to thrive in heart failure
    • Why we cluster care, keep infants warm, and limit feeds to about 30 minutes
    C10

    Obstruction raises pressure upstream

    A narrowing raises pressure before it and lowers pressure and flow after it. The chamber pumping against it thickens (hypertrophy).

    Before narrowing: ↑ pressure · After narrowing: ↓ pressure and flow

    Picture it: Put your thumb over a hose: pressure builds behind your thumb and less water comes out the end.

    Where you'll see it
    • Coarctation: high BP in the arms, weak pulses and low BP in the legs
    • Tetralogy of Fallot: pulmonary stenosis causes right ventricular hypertrophy
    • Pressure behind an obstruction can push blood through any available opening

    A&P: fetal circulation and the child's heart

    Before birth the lungs are filled with fluid and the placenta does the breathing. The fetal heart uses three shortcuts to send blood around the lungs. Most congenital defects make sense once you know these shortcuts and what happens to them at birth.

    Normal blood flow, quickly

    Body → vena cavae → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → body

    The right side pumps unoxygenated (blue) blood to the lungs at low pressure. The left side pumps oxygenated (red) blood to the body at high pressure. After birth, pressures on the left side are higher than on the right. That fact explains the direction of most shunts.

    Three fetal shortcuts

    ShortcutConnectsPurpose before birthAfter birth
    Ductus venosusUmbilical vein to inferior vena cavaSends oxygenated placental blood past the liverCloses within days; becomes a ligament
    Foramen ovaleRight atrium to left atriumSends oxygenated blood straight to the left heart and brain, skipping the lungsFlap closes functionally within hours when left atrial pressure rises
    Ductus arteriosusPulmonary artery to aortaSends blood leaving the right ventricle into the aorta instead of the high-resistance lungsConstricts within hours to days as oxygen rises and prostaglandins fall; sealed by 2–3 weeks

    What changes at the first breath

    1. The lungs expand and fill with air. Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) drops sharply, and blood flows freely into the lungs.
    2. The umbilical cord is clamped. The low-resistance placenta is gone, so systemic vascular resistance (SVR) rises.
    3. More blood returns from the lungs to the left atrium. Left atrial pressure rises above right atrial pressure, pushing the foramen ovale flap closed.
    4. Rising oxygen levels and falling prostaglandins make the ductus arteriosus constrict.

    PVR keeps falling over the first 6 to 8 weeks of life. This is why some defects don't cause symptoms until a baby is 1 to 2 months old.

    Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)

    The ductus arteriosus fails to close after birth. Blood shunts from the high-pressure aorta back into the low-pressure pulmonary artery, sending extra blood to the lungs.

    Who and why

    • Very common in premature infants (immature ductus, less response to oxygen)
    • Also linked to maternal rubella and high altitude
    • Classification: increased pulmonary blood flow; acyanotic

    Findings

    • May have no symptoms if small
    • Continuous "machinery" murmur at the upper left sternal border
    • Bounding pulses and wide pulse pressure (blood runs off into the lungs during diastole)
    • Heart failure signs if large; risk of endocarditis

    Treatment

    • Medication to close it (preterm infants): IV indomethacin or ibuprofen, which block prostaglandins. Acetaminophen is sometimes used. Monitor urine output, renal function, platelets and bleeding, and feeding tolerance.
    • Procedure: transcatheter device or coil closure in the cardiac catheterization lab, or surgical ligation.
    Sometimes the ductus must stay open

    In ductal-dependent defects (such as transposition of the great arteries or severe coarctation), the ductus is the baby's lifeline. Prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil) by continuous IV infusion keeps it open until surgery. The key adverse effect is apnea, so keep the infant on a cardiorespiratory monitor with airway equipment ready. Fever, flushing, and hypotension can also occur.

    Atrial septal defect (ASD)

    An opening in the wall between the atria. Because left atrial pressure is higher, blood shunts left to right, adding volume to the right heart and the lungs.

    Findings

    • Often no symptoms; may be found during a well-child exam or later in life
    • Systolic murmur at the upper left sternal border with a fixed split S2
    • Large defects: fatigue, frequent respiratory infections, rarely heart failure in infancy
    • Long-term risks if unrepaired: atrial dysrhythmias, pulmonary hypertension, stroke from a clot crossing the defect

    Treatment

    • Small secundum defects often close on their own in the first years
    • Transcatheter device closure in the cath lab for many secundum ASDs
    • Surgical patch or suture closure for large or other types
    • Outcomes after repair are excellent
    After cardiac catheterization

    Check the insertion-site dressing for bleeding, and the pulse, color, warmth, and capillary refill of the affected leg, which is the most important assessment. Keep the leg straight for the ordered time (often 4–6 hours). Monitor vital signs, especially heart rate for dysrhythmias, and encourage fluids to clear contrast. If bleeding occurs, apply direct continuous pressure 1 inch above the site and notify the provider.

    Ventricular septal defect (VSD)

    The most common congenital heart defect: an opening in the wall between the ventricles. The high-pressure left ventricle pushes blood into the right ventricle and on to the lungs.

    Findings

    • Loud, harsh holosystolic murmur at the lower left sternal border
    • Small VSD: murmur only
    • Large VSD: heart failure signs that often appear at 4–8 weeks as pulmonary resistance falls and the shunt grows
    • Risk of endocarditis

    Treatment

    • Many small muscular VSDs close on their own, often in the first year
    • Treat heart failure with medications and high-calorie feeds
    • Surgical patch closure (or transcatheter device for some)
    • Pulmonary artery banding is sometimes a temporary step in small infants
    Why repair can't wait too long

    Years of extra flow damage the pulmonary vessels and raise pulmonary resistance. If right-sided pressure climbs above left, the shunt reverses to right to left and the child becomes cyanotic. This is Eisenmenger syndrome, and at that point the defect can no longer be closed safely.

    Coarctation of the aorta (CoA)

    A narrowing of the aorta, usually near where the ductus arteriosus attaches. Pressure builds up before the narrowing (head and arms) and drops after it (lower body and legs).

    Findings

    • High BP and bounding pulses in the arms
    • Weak or absent femoral pulses, cool lower extremities, lower BP in the legs (normally leg BP is equal to or higher than arm BP)
    • Older children: headaches, dizziness, nosebleeds, leg cramps with exercise
    • Severe in newborns: heart failure or shock when the ductus closes
    • Associated with Turner syndrome and bicuspid aortic valve

    Treatment

    • Severe newborn coarctation: prostaglandin E1 to reopen the ductus until surgery
    • Surgical resection with end-to-end anastomosis (common in infants)
    • Balloon angioplasty with or without a stent (older children and re-narrowing)
    • Lifelong BP follow-up; hypertension can persist
    Assessment habit

    Compare upper and lower extremity pulses and blood pressures (four-extremity BP). After repair, watch for rebound hypertension (treated with IV antihypertensives), bleeding, and abdominal pain or distention from changes in bowel blood flow. Check that leg pulses are now strong.

    Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)

    The most common cyanotic defect. Four problems occur together, but the severity of one, pulmonary stenosis, decides how blue the child is.

    The four defectsEffect
    Pulmonary stenosisBlocks blood from leaving the right ventricle to reach the lungs
    Right ventricular hypertrophyThe RV thickens from pumping against the obstruction
    Overriding aortaThe aorta sits over the VSD and receives blood from both ventricles
    Ventricular septal defectLets blue blood cross from the right ventricle into the aorta

    Memory aid: PROV (Pulmonary stenosis, Right ventricular hypertrophy, Overriding aorta, VSD).

    Findings

    • Cyanosis, which may be mild at birth and worsens as the infant grows or the stenosis tightens
    • Systolic murmur from the pulmonary stenosis
    • Chronic hypoxemia: clubbing, polycythemia (raises risk of stroke and clots, especially with dehydration), poor growth
    • Older unrepaired children squat after exertion; squatting raises SVR and pushes more blood to the lungs
    • Chest x-ray: boot-shaped heart

    Hypercyanotic ("tet") spells

    Sudden episodes of deeper cyanosis, often triggered by crying, feeding, a bowel movement, or waking. The infant becomes irritable, breathes rapidly and deeply, turns very blue, and may go limp or lose consciousness. The murmur may get quieter because less blood is crossing the pulmonary valve.

    Managing a tet spell

    1. Place the infant in the knee-chest position (hold the baby against your shoulder with knees tucked to chest); this raises SVR and sends more blood to the lungs. 2. Stay calm and soothe the infant. 3. Give 100% oxygen. 4. Give morphine as ordered to relax the infundibulum and slow breathing. 5. IV fluids and other medications (such as propranolol or phenylephrine) as ordered. Teach parents the knee-chest position.

    Treatment

    • Complete repair in the first year of life: close the VSD and widen the right ventricular outflow tract.
    • Palliative shunt (modified Blalock-Taussig: a tube from a systemic artery to the pulmonary artery) for infants too small or unstable for full repair.
    • Keep the child well hydrated, prevent and treat anemia, and give endocarditis prophylaxis as ordered.

    Transposition of the great arteries (TGA)

    The aorta and pulmonary artery are switched. The aorta leaves the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery leaves the left ventricle. The result is two separate circuits that never meet.

    Why it's an emergency

    • Blue blood from the body goes RA → RV → aorta → back to the body without passing through the lungs
    • Red blood goes LA → LV → pulmonary artery → back to the lungs
    • Survival depends on mixing through a PDA, foramen ovale or ASD, or VSD
    • As the ductus and foramen ovale close, the newborn becomes profoundly cyanotic

    Findings

    • Cyanosis at birth or within hours that does not improve with oxygen
    • Tachypnea without much respiratory distress at first
    • Murmur may be absent
    • Heart failure signs if a large VSD is present
    • Chest x-ray: "egg on a string" heart shape

    Treatment

    • Prostaglandin E1 infusion to keep the ductus open (watch for apnea).
    • Balloon atrial septostomy (Rashkind procedure) during cardiac catheterization to enlarge the atrial opening and improve mixing.
    • Arterial switch operation, usually within the first 2 weeks of life: the aorta and pulmonary artery are moved to the correct ventricles, and the coronary arteries are reattached to the new aorta.
    • After surgery: monitor for low cardiac output, dysrhythmias, bleeding, and signs of coronary ischemia.
    Supporting the family

    Parents may go from a routine birth to a transfer to a cardiac center within hours. Explain what is happening in simple terms, encourage holding and touch when the infant is stable, support breast milk expression, and prepare them for the intensive care environment.

      The conduction system

      Specialized cells generate an impulse and carry it along a set path, so the atria contract first and the ventricles contract together a fraction of a second later.

      StructureLocationJobIntrinsic rate
      SA nodeUpper right atriumThe normal pacemaker; starts each beat60–100/min

      AV nodeFloor of the right atrium near the septumDelays the impulse about 0.1 second so the atria can empty into the ventricles; backup pacemaker40–60/min
      Bundle of HisTop of the septumCarries the impulse from the AV node into the ventricles40–60/min (junction)

      Four properties of cardiac cells

      PropertyMeaning
      AutomaticityCan start an impulse on its own, without a signal from the nerves
      ExcitabilityCan respond to an impulse
      ConductivityCan pass the impulse to the next cell
      ContractilityCan shorten (squeeze) in response to the impulse
      Nerves adjust the rate

      The heart starts its own beats, but the autonomic nervous system adjusts the speed. Sympathetic stimulation (norepinephrine, epinephrine) speeds the rate and strengthens contraction. Parasympathetic stimulation through the vagus nerve slows the rate. This is why vagal maneuvers can slow SVT and why atropine, which blocks the vagus, speeds a slow heart.

      Depolarization and repolarization

      At rest, the inside of a cardiac cell is negative. When ions rush across the membrane the charge flips, the cell fires and contracts, and then pumps restore the resting state.

      PhaseWhat happensResult
      Resting (polarized)Inside negative; potassium is high inside, sodium high outsideCell ready to fire
      DepolarizationSodium rushes in (and calcium follows); the inside becomes positiveElectrical activation, then contraction
      PlateauCalcium keeps flowing inSustains the contraction
      RepolarizationPotassium moves out; the inside returns to negativeMuscle relaxes and resets

      Refractory periods

      Absolute refractory period

      • The cell cannot respond to any impulse
      • From the start of the QRS to about the peak of the T wave
      • Protects the heart from beats that come too soon

      Relative refractory period

      • A strong enough impulse can fire the cell
      • The downslope of the T wave (the "vulnerable period")
      • An early beat here (R-on-T) can trigger ventricular fibrillation

      Key concepts

      These principles explain why rhythms look and behave the way they do. Section 4 builds on every one of them.

      C1

      The fastest pacemaker wins

      Whichever site fires fastest controls the heart. The SA node normally wins; if it fails or slows, a slower backup site (AV junction, then ventricles) takes over.

      SA 60–100 > junction 40–60 > ventricles 20–40

      Picture it: In a relay race, whoever reaches the baton first runs the next leg.

      Where you'll see it
      • Junctional and idioventricular rhythms are escape rhythms
      • Atrial tachycardia overrides the SA node
      • Why third-degree block produces a slow ventricular rate
      C2

      Electrical comes before mechanical

      The ECG shows only electricity. Contraction must follow, but it doesn't always. A rhythm on the monitor doesn't prove there is a pulse.

      Electrical impulse → contraction → pulse

      Picture it: A light switch can flip even when the bulb is burned out.

      Where you'll see it
      • Pulseless electrical activity (PEA)
      • Always check the patient before trusting the monitor
      • Checking for a pulse with a paced rhythm
      C3

      Toward the positive electrode writes upward

      Electricity moving toward a lead's positive electrode makes an upward deflection; moving away makes a downward one.

      Toward + → upright · Away from + → inverted

      Picture it: A car coming toward you gets bigger; one driving away gets smaller.

      Where you'll see it
      • Lead II shows upright P waves and QRS complexes
      • An inverted P wave in lead II means the impulse started low (junctional)
      • Wide, bizarre QRS shapes from ventricular beats
      C4

      Refractory periods protect the rhythm

      After firing, a cell can't fire again right away. This keeps the heart from beating too fast, but the end of the T wave is a vulnerable window.

      Absolute: QRS to T peak · Relative: T downslope

      Picture it: A camera flash needs a moment to recharge before it can fire again.

      Where you'll see it
      • R-on-T PVCs can trigger VF
      • Synchronized cardioversion avoids the T wave
      • Long QT raises the risk of torsades
      C5

      Potassium sets the resting charge

      The potassium gradient across the cell membrane sets how ready cells are to fire. Too low or too high a serum potassium makes the heart irritable or slow.

      ↓ K⁺: irritable (PVCs, VT) · ↑ K⁺: peaked T, wide QRS, block, arrest

      Picture it: A bow drawn too loose won't shoot; drawn too tight, it fires on its own.

      Where you'll see it
      • Monitoring potassium in clients on diuretics or digoxin
      • Peaked T waves in kidney failure
      • Never giving potassium IV push
      C6

      The AV node delay lets the ventricles fill

      The AV node holds each impulse about 0.1 second so the atria finish emptying before the ventricles contract. It also filters very fast atrial impulses.

      PR interval 0.12–0.20 sec

      Picture it: A traffic light that holds cars for a moment so the intersection clears.

      Where you'll see it
      • First-degree block lengthens the delay
      • In atrial fibrillation the AV node limits how many impulses reach the ventricles
      • Rate-control drugs work at the AV node

      Leads and cardiac monitoring

      A lead is a point of view: a negative and a positive electrode looking at the heart's electricity from one angle. Most bedside monitors show lead II, which lines up with the normal path of the impulse.

      Einthoven's three limb leads

      • Lead I: right arm (−) to left arm (+)
      • Lead II: right arm (−) to left leg (+)
      • Lead III: left arm (−) to left leg (+)
      • The impulse travels from the SA node (upper right) toward the apex (lower left), almost exactly along lead II, so lead II shows tall, upright P waves and QRS complexes

      The 12-lead ECG

      • Adds the augmented limb leads (aVR, aVL, aVF) and six chest leads (V1–V6)
      • Twelve views locate ischemia and infarction by wall: inferior (II, III, aVF), anterior (V1–V4), lateral (I, aVL, V5–V6)
      • Done within 10 minutes of arrival for chest pain

        The strip shows electricity; only the patient shows perfusion. Always check responsiveness, pulse, BP, and symptoms before acting.

        Picture it: A smoke alarm can go off from burned toast; you check for fire before evacuating.

        • Artifact that looks like VF

        • Deciding whether a bradycardia needs treatment

        Unstable means the rhythm is causing hypotension, altered mental status, signs of shock, chest pain, or acute heart failure. Unstable rhythms get electricity; stable ones usually get medications first.

        Unstable fast → cardioversion · Unstable slow → atropine, pacing

        Picture it: A small leak gets a patch; a burst pipe gets the water shut off now.

          Key concepts

          These principles explain chest pain, ECG changes, and why every minute counts.

          C1

          Oxygen supply vs. demand

          Angina happens when the heart's oxygen demand outruns what narrowed coronary arteries can deliver. Lower the demand or raise the supply and the pain eases.

          Demand ↑: HR, BP, contractility · Supply ↓: narrowing, clot, spasm, anemia

          Picture it: A narrow road handles light traffic but jams at rush hour.

          Where you'll see it
          • Stable angina with exertion
          • Rest, nitroglycerin, and beta blockers
          • Anemia or tachycardia worsening angina
          C2

          The coronaries fill in diastole

          Coronary arteries fill mainly when the heart relaxes. Fast heart rates and low diastolic pressure cut coronary flow.

          ↑ HR → ↓ diastolic time → ↓ coronary flow

          Picture it: You can't refill a squeezed water bottle; it fills only when you let go.

          Where you'll see it
          • Beta blockers protect ischemic muscle by slowing the rate
          • Tachycardia can trigger angina
          • Hypotension lowers coronary perfusion
          C3

          Time is muscle

          After complete blockage, muscle starts to die within about 20–40 minutes and death spreads over hours. The sooner flow returns, the more muscle is saved.

          ECG in 10 min · Door-to-balloon ≤ 90 min · Door-to-needle ≤ 30 min

          Picture it: A house fire spreads room by room; the faster the fire truck arrives, the more rooms are saved.

          Where you'll see it
          • Recognizing atypical symptoms quickly
          • STEMI time goals
          • Teaching clients to call 911, not drive themselves
          C4

          Ischemia, injury, infarction

          Ischemic tissue is starved but alive and reversible. Injured tissue is in danger. Infarcted tissue is dead. ECG and troponin track the progression.

          Ischemia: ST depression, T inversion · Injury: ST elevation · Infarction: Q waves, troponin

          Picture it: A plant without water wilts first, then browns, then dies; water it early and it recovers.

          Where you'll see it
          • Why an early ECG can be normal
          • Why troponin rises only with muscle death
          • Saving the injured zone with reperfusion
          C5

          Plaque rupture makes a clot

          A stable plaque narrows the artery slowly. When its cap ruptures, platelets stick and a clot forms within minutes, turning stable CAD into ACS.

          Rupture → platelets → thrombus → partial (UA/NSTEMI) or complete (STEMI) blockage

          Picture it: A crack in a dam can be stable for years; a sudden breach floods the valley.

          Where you'll see it
          • Why aspirin and antiplatelets are first-line
          • Why statins stabilize plaque
          • Why unstable angina is an emergency
          C6

          The right ventricle depends on preload

          A right ventricle damaged by an inferior MI can't push blood forward on its own. It depends on volume coming in, so drugs that lower preload can drop the BP sharply.

          RV infarct: hypotension + JVD + clear lungs → avoid nitrates, give fluids

          Picture it: A weak pump can still move water if the tank above it stays full.

          Where you'll see it
          • Inferior MI with hypotension
          • Holding nitroglycerin and morphine
          • Giving IV fluid boluses

          Coronary artery disease and stable angina

          Coronary artery disease (CAD) is atherosclerosis in the coronary arteries. A stable plaque narrows the artery; when demand rises, supply can't keep up and the client feels angina.

          Risk factors

          Modifiable

          • Smoking
          • Hypertension
          • High LDL, low HDL
          • Diabetes
          • Obesity, inactivity, diet high in saturated fat
          • Stress

          Nonmodifiable

          • Age
          • Male sex; women's risk rises after menopause
          • Family history of early heart disease
          • Ethnicity and genetics

          Types of angina

          TypePatternRelief
          Stable anginaPredictable chest pressure with exertion, cold, a heavy meal, or stress; lasts a few minutesRest or nitroglycerin within minutes
          Unstable anginaNew, at rest, or more frequent, longer, or harder to relieveNot reliably relieved; this is ACS
          Variant (Prinzmetal) anginaCoronary spasm at rest, often early morning; triggers include smoking, cocaine, and cold; brief ST elevation that resolvesCalcium channel blockers and nitrates

          Managing stable CAD

          • Nitroglycerin SL for attacks; long-acting nitrates to prevent them. Headache is common; check BP.
          • Beta blockers lower heart rate and oxygen demand.
          • Calcium channel blockers relax coronary arteries; first choice for variant angina.
          • Aspirin and statins for every client with CAD unless contraindicated.
          • Lifestyle: stop smoking, heart-healthy diet, regular exercise, BP and glucose control.

          Acute coronary syndrome

          When a plaque ruptures, platelets pile on and a clot forms. How completely the clot blocks the artery decides which ACS it is.

          BlockageTroponinECGUrgency
          Unstable anginaPartial; no muscle deathNormalMay show ST depression or T-wave inversionAdmit, monitor, treat

          Recognizing ACS

          Classic symptoms

          • Chest pressure, squeezing, or heaviness lasting more than a few minutes
          • Pain radiating to the left arm, jaw, neck, or back
          • Shortness of breath, diaphoresis, nausea, anxiety ("sense of doom")

          Atypical presentations

          • More common in women, older adults, and people with diabetes
          • Fatigue, shortness of breath, nausea, indigestion
          • Jaw, back, or upper abdominal discomfort
          • Diabetic neuropathy can blunt chest pain
          Troponin and timing

          Troponin rises within hours of injury, so an early value can be normal. It is drawn serially. In a STEMI, the ECG, not the troponin, decides the treatment.

          Emergency care and reperfusion

          The first 10 minutes set the course. Get the ECG, give aspirin, relieve pain safely, and activate reperfusion if it's a STEMI.

          The first 10 minutes

          Mr. James Whitaker, 63, has crushing chest pain. The ECG shows ST elevation in the inferior leads. BP 88/56, HR 52. Lungs are clear, but JVD is present at 45°. He asks for "one of those under-the-tongue pills."

          Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG)

          CABG bypasses blocked arteries with a vein (saphenous) or artery (internal mammary) graft when disease is widespread or PCI isn't possible. Early care focuses on bleeding (chest tube output over 100 mL/hr is usually reported), cardiac tamponade, dysrhythmias (especially atrial fibrillation), BP control, rewarming, glucose control, and pain control so the client can cough and deep breathe.             A&P of the vascular system

            Arteries are built to handle pressure. Veins are built to hold volume. Capillaries are built for exchange. Each disease in this unit breaks one of those designs.

            Three layers, three vessel types

            Tunica externa (adventitia)Tunica mediaTunica intima (endothelium)
            LayerWhat it isWhy it matters clinically
            Tunica intimaEndothelium, a single cell layer lining every vesselWhere atherosclerosis begins; injury here triggers clotting and inflammation; tears here start an aortic dissection
            Tunica mediaSmooth muscle and elastic fibersControls vessel diameter (constriction and dilation); weakens in aneurysms; stiffens with age and hypertension
            Tunica externaConnective tissue and collagenAnchors the vessel and resists overstretching

            Arteries vs. veins

            Arterial side

            Delivery under pressure

            • Carry blood away from the heart
            • Elastic large arteries absorb each beat and recoil to keep flow moving
            • Arterioles are the main control point for resistance
            • Problem when blocked: tissue is starved of oxygen (ischemia)
            Venous side

            Return against gravity

            • Carry blood back to the heart
            • Hold about 60 to 70% of total blood volume (capacitance vessels)
            • One-way valves plus the calf muscle pump push blood upward
            • Problem when blocked or valves fail: blood pools, causing edema and congestion

            What sets blood pressure

            BP = Cardiac output × Systemic vascular resistance
            Cardiac output = Heart rate × Stroke volume

            Anything that raises heart rate, stroke volume, or resistance raises blood pressure. The body adjusts all three constantly:

            • Baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch sense stretch. High BP leads to slower heart rate and vasodilation; low BP leads to the opposite.
            • Sympathetic nervous system releases norepinephrine, which raises heart rate and constricts vessels.
            • RAAS: low renal perfusion releases renin → angiotensin I → (ACE) → angiotensin II. Angiotensin II is a powerful vasoconstrictor and triggers aldosterone, which retains sodium and water.
            • ADH (vasopressin) retains water and constricts vessels.
            • Endothelium releases nitric oxide (vasodilator) and endothelin (vasoconstrictor) to fine-tune local flow.

            Mean arterial pressure (MAP) is the average pressure pushing blood into the organs during one cardiac cycle: MAP = (SBP + 2 × DBP) ÷ 3. A MAP of at least 65 mm Hg is generally needed to perfuse the brain, heart, and kidneys.

            Key concepts

            These principles explain why the findings in the next sections happen. Learn them once and you can reason through conditions you haven't memorized.

            C1

            Flow, pressure, and resistance

            Blood pressure rises when the heart pumps more or the vessels resist more. Lower either one and pressure falls.

            BP = Cardiac output × Systemic vascular resistance

            Picture it: A garden hose sprays harder when you open the faucet more (cardiac output) or squeeze the nozzle (resistance).

            Where you'll see it
            • Each antihypertensive class lowers one part: diuretics and beta blockers lower output; ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and calcium channel blockers lower resistance
            • Aortic dissection: beta blockers lower the force of each beat before vasodilators lower resistance
            • Shock: low output or lost resistance drops BP
            C2

            Poiseuille's law: radius rules flow

            Flow through a tube depends on its radius raised to the fourth power. A small narrowing causes a large drop in flow.

            Flow ∝ radius⁴ · halve the radius → about 1/16 of the flow

            Picture it: Pinch a drinking straw halfway closed and it becomes very hard to drink through.

            Where you'll see it
            • Peripheral arterial disease: plaque that narrows the artery moderately can cut flow enough to cause claudication
            • Carotid and renal artery stenosis
            • Why small changes in arteriole diameter control blood pressure so powerfully
            C3

            Laplace's law: bigger vessels, more wall stress

            The tension on a vessel wall rises with both the pressure inside it and its radius. As a vessel widens, its wall is under more stress, so it widens further.

            Wall tension ∝ Pressure × Radius

            Picture it: A balloon is hardest to blow up at the start. Once it's big, it stretches easily, and that's when it pops.

            Where you'll see it
            • Abdominal aortic aneurysm: rupture risk climbs as the diameter grows, which is why size guides repair
            • Why BP control matters for aneurysm and dissection
            • Dilated ventricles in heart failure work harder
            C4

            Gravity and hydrostatic pressure

            Gravity pulls blood toward the lowest part of the body. Positioning can help or hinder flow.

            Picture it: Water flows downhill. Pumping it uphill takes valves and muscle.

            Where you'll see it
            • Peripheral arterial disease: dangling the legs helps arterial blood reach the feet
            • Venous insufficiency: elevating the legs helps blood return to the heart
            • Dependent edema; venous stasis as a cause of DVT
            C5

            Starling forces at the capillary

            Hydrostatic pressure pushes fluid out of capillaries. Oncotic pressure from plasma proteins (mainly albumin) pulls it back in. Edema forms when push beats pull.

            Push out: capillary hydrostatic pressure · Pull in: plasma oncotic pressure

            Picture it: A soaker hose leaks more when you turn up the water pressure. A sponge (albumin) next to it soaks some of the water back up.

            Where you'll see it
            • Venous insufficiency: high venous pressure pushes fluid into the legs
            • Pulmonary edema in hypertensive emergency and fluid overload
            • Low albumin (liver disease, malnutrition) causes edema even with normal pressure
            C6

            Turbulent flow

            Blood normally flows smoothly in layers. A narrowing or a bulge disrupts the layers, creating turbulence you can hear (bruit) or feel (thrill).

            Picture it: A calm river is quiet. Where it narrows over rocks, the rapids roar.

            Where you'll see it
            • Carotid bruit in atherosclerosis
            • Abdominal bruit over an aneurysm
            • Turbulence at rough plaque also activates platelets and promotes clots
            C7

            Daily weight tracks fluid

            Daily weight is the most reliable indicator of fluid gain or loss. A change of 1 kg (2.2 lb) is about 1 liter of fluid.

            1 kg ≈ 1 L of fluid

            Picture it: A liter bottle of water weighs about a kilogram. Gaining a kilogram overnight is like drinking that bottle and keeping it.

            Where you'll see it
            • Clients on diuretics for hypertension
            • Heart failure from long-standing hypertension
            • Kidney injury after AAA repair; fluid overload during transfusion
            • Weigh at the same time, on the same scale, in similar clothing, usually after voiding and before breakfast

            In chronic hypertension the brain and kidneys have reset their autoregulation to expect high pressure. Dropping BP too quickly can cause stroke, MI, or kidney injury. The usual target: reduce MAP by no more than 25% in the first hour, then toward 160/100–110 over the next 2 to 6 hours, then toward normal over 24 to 48 hours. Exceptions include aortic dissection (lower fast, see the Aortic dissection page) and acute ischemic stroke (follows its own targets).

            Nursing care: BP every 5 to 15 minutes during titration, continuous cardiac monitoring, neuro checks, hourly urine output, and watch for hypotension.

            Peripheral arterial disease

            PAD is atherosclerosis in the arteries of the legs (most often). Oxygen supply can't keep up with demand, so muscles and skin starve. Think: blood can't get in.

            Assessment findings

            • Intermittent claudication: cramping pain in the calf, thigh, or buttock with walking that goes away after a few minutes of rest. Pain occurs at a predictable distance.
            • Rest pain: burning pain in the foot at rest, often at night, relieved by dangling the leg. This means advanced disease.
            • Diminished or absent pedal and posterior tibial pulses
            • Cool, pale skin; elevation pallor and dependent rubor
            • Thin, shiny, hairless skin; thick, brittle toenails; slow capillary refill
            • Ulcers on toes, between toes, heels, or the lateral malleolus: pale, deep, round "punched-out," little drainage, very painful

            Ankle-brachial index (ABI)

            The ABI compares ankle systolic pressure to arm systolic pressure, measured with a Doppler. In healthy legs the ankle pressure is equal to or slightly higher than the arm. ABI = ankle SBP ÷ brachial SBP (use the higher arm reading).

            Management and nursing care

            • Smoking cessation is the single most important intervention.
            • Supervised walking program: walk until claudication pain starts, rest until it stops, repeat, about 30 to 45 minutes, 3 times a week. This builds collateral circulation.
            • Position: legs level or slightly dependent. Do not elevate the legs; gravity helps arterial blood reach the feet.
            • Avoid crossing legs and tight clothing; keep warm with socks, but no heating pads (reduced sensation means burns).
            • Daily foot inspection, well-fitting shoes, professional nail care.
            • Medications: antiplatelet (aspirin, clopidogrel), statin, BP and glucose control, cilostazol for claudication (contraindicated in heart failure).
            • Revascularization: angioplasty with stent, atherectomy, or bypass graft.
            Emergency: acute arterial occlusion

            A sudden clot or embolus blocks a limb artery. Watch for the six P's: pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia (cold). Notify the provider immediately. This is also what you watch for after a revascularization procedure, along with bleeding at the access site and compartment syndrome.

            Chronic venous insufficiency

            When vein valves stop closing properly, blood falls backward and pools in the lower legs. Pressure builds in the veins and pushes fluid and red cells into the tissue. Think: blood can't get out.

            Pathophysiology

            Damaged valves (often after a DVT) or chronically stretched veins → reflux → venous hypertension in the lower leg → capillaries leak fluid, protein, and red blood cells into the tissue.

            Those red cells break down and leave iron behind as hemosiderin, which stains the skin brown around the ankles. Over time the skin hardens and breaks down.

            Risk factors: previous DVT, varicose veins, obesity, pregnancy, prolonged standing or sitting, older age, female sex, family history.

            Assessment findings

            • Edema of the lower legs that worsens through the day and improves with elevation
            • Aching, heaviness, or fullness, relieved by elevating the legs
            • Brown or bronze discoloration (hemosiderin staining) around the ankles
            • Stasis dermatitis: itchy, scaly, thickened skin
            • Pulses present (they may be hard to feel through edema); legs warm
            • Varicose veins: dilated, twisted superficial veins
            • Ulcers near the medial malleolus: shallow, irregular edges, red granular base, moderate to heavy drainage, less painful than arterial ulcers

            Management and nursing care

            • Elevate legs above heart level for 20 to 30 minutes several times a day and when sleeping.
            • Compression stockings or wraps: apply in the morning before getting out of bed, before edema builds. Rule out arterial disease first (check ABI); compression on ischemic legs causes harm.
            • Walking activates the calf muscle pump. Avoid prolonged standing or sitting and crossing legs.
            • Venous ulcer care: moist wound healing, compression (Unna boot or multilayer wrap), treat infection.
            • Skin care: moisturize, protect from trauma, inspect daily.

            Venous thromboembolism (VTE)

            VTE covers two linked conditions: a deep vein thrombosis (DVT), a clot in a deep vein (usually the leg), and a pulmonary embolism (PE), when part of that clot breaks off and lodges in the lungs.

            Virchow's triad

            Any one of these raises clot risk. Most hospitalized clients have at least two.

            FactorExamples
            Venous stasisImmobility, bed rest, long flights or car rides, paralysis, heart failure, obesity, pregnancy
            Vessel wall injurySurgery (especially hip, knee, pelvic), trauma, fractures, IV catheters, central lines
            HypercoagulabilityCancer, estrogen (oral contraceptives, hormone therapy), pregnancy, dehydration, smoking, inherited clotting disorders, sepsis

            Recognizing DVT and PE

            DVT

            Usually one leg

            • Unilateral swelling (measure and compare calf circumference)
            • Pain or tenderness, warmth, redness
            • Many DVTs have no symptoms
            • Homans' sign is not reliable; don't use it
            • Diagnosed with D-dimer and venous duplex ultrasound
            PE

            Sudden, and an emergency

            • Sudden dyspnea and tachypnea (most common)
            • Pleuritic chest pain
            • Tachycardia, anxiety, sense of impending doom
            • Low SpO₂, cough, possibly hemoptysis
            • Diagnosed with CT pulmonary angiography
            Never massage the leg

            Massaging or rubbing a leg with a suspected DVT can dislodge the clot and send it to the lungs. If PE is suspected: stay with the client, raise the head of the bed, give oxygen, and call a rapid response.

            Anticoagulants: what they do and don't do

            Anticoagulants prevent the clot from growing and prevent new clots. They do not dissolve the existing clot; the body's own fibrinolytic system does that over time. Thrombolytics (alteplase) dissolve clots and are reserved for massive PE.

            Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)

            An aneurysm is a permanent bulge where the arterial wall has weakened. Most aortic aneurysms form in the abdominal aorta below the renal arteries. They grow slowly and silently until they are found or they rupture.

            Who gets one

            • Men over 65, especially those who have ever smoked (smoking is the biggest risk factor)
            • Atherosclerosis and hypertension weaken the media
            • Family history; connective tissue disorders (Marfan syndrome)
            • Screening: a one-time abdominal ultrasound is recommended for men 65 to 75 who have ever smoked

            Assessment

            Intact AAA

            • Often no symptoms; found on imaging for something else
            • Pulsating mass near or slightly left of the umbilicus
            • Abdominal bruit
            • Vague abdominal or low back pain
            • Do not palpate deeply
            Rupture

            Surgical emergency

            • Sudden, severe back, flank, or abdominal pain
            • Hypotension, tachycardia, signs of shock
            • Flank bruising (retroperitoneal bleed)
            • Decreasing level of consciousness
            • High mortality: get help immediately

            Management

            • Small aneurysms: monitor size with regular ultrasound or CT; control BP; stop smoking; statin.
            • Repair is usually recommended around 5.5 cm in men (smaller in women), or if it grows quickly or causes symptoms.
            • EVAR (endovascular aneurysm repair): a stent graft placed through the femoral arteries. Shorter recovery.
            • Open repair: abdominal incision, aorta clamped, graft sewn in.
            Post-op priorities

            Graft patency and bleeding: peripheral pulses, color, warmth, and sensation of both legs; access site for bleeding or hematoma (EVAR); back pain or falling BP can mean graft leak. Kidneys: hourly urine output and creatinine, since the aorta was clamped near the renal arteries or contrast was used. BP control: high pressure stresses the suture lines; low pressure threatens graft flow.

            Aortic dissection

            A tear in the intima lets blood surge into the wall of the aorta, splitting the layers apart and creating a false channel. Each heartbeat can push the tear further. It is not the same as an aneurysm, though the two can occur together.

            Risk factors

            Hypertension (the most common), Marfan and other connective tissue disorders, bicuspid aortic valve, cocaine or amphetamine use, pregnancy, trauma, and prior heart surgery.

            Assessment

            • Sudden, severe chest or back pain often described as tearing or ripping, at its worst right from the start
            • Pain may move as the dissection extends (chest to back to abdomen)
            • Blood pressure difference of more than 20 mm Hg between arms; unequal pulses
            • Signs depend on which branches are cut off: stroke symptoms, MI, absent leg pulses, abdominal pain, decreased urine output
            • Type A can cause a new aortic regurgitation murmur or cardiac tamponade
            • Diagnosed with CT angiography or transesophageal echo

            Stanford classification

            Type A

            Involves the ascending aorta

            • Can tear into the coronary arteries, aortic valve, or pericardium
            • Emergency surgery
            • Highest mortality
            Type B

            Descending aorta only

            • Usually managed medically: BP and HR control, pain control
            • Endovascular stent if complications develop (organ ischemia, rupture risk)

            Priority actions

            Lower heart rate first, then pressure

            The goal is to reduce the force of each ejection against the torn wall. Give an IV beta blocker first (esmolol, labetalol) to bring heart rate to about 60 or below. Then lower systolic BP to roughly 100 to 120 mm Hg, adding a vasodilator such as nicardipine or nitroprusside if needed. A vasodilator given alone first causes reflex tachycardia, which makes the tear worse. Unlike other hypertensive emergencies, BP is lowered quickly here.

            • Pain control with IV opioids (pain drives up HR and BP)
            • Two large-bore IVs, arterial line, continuous monitoring, BP in both arms
            • Frequent neuro checks, pulses in all four extremities, urine output
            • Keep the client calm and on bed rest; prepare for surgery if Type A

              Key concepts

              These principles explain every type of shock. Learn the model and you can reason through any client.

              C1

              Perfusion needs pressure and flow

              Organs need both enough pressure (MAP) and enough flow (cardiac output) to receive oxygen. Shock is a failure of one or both.

              MAP = CO × SVR · Goal MAP ≥ 65 mm Hg

              Picture it: A garden needs both water pressure and an open hose to stay green.

              Where you'll see it

              C2

              The tank, the pump, and the pipes

              Every shock comes from an empty tank (volume), a failing pump (heart), pipes that are too wide (vasodilation), or a blockage. Find which one and you know what to fix.

              Hypovolemic: tank · Cardiogenic: pump · Distributive: pipes · Obstructive: blockage

              Picture it: A home water system fails if the tank runs dry, the pump breaks, the pipes burst open, or something clogs the line.

              Where you'll see it
              • Choosing fluids vs. inotropes vs. vasopressors
              • Reading JVD and lung sounds
              • Classifying any shock client
              C3

              Compensation hides early shock

              The sympathetic nervous system and RAAS raise heart rate, constrict vessels, and hold fluid to keep BP normal. BP is often the last vital sign to fall.

              Early: ↑ HR, ↑ RR, narrow pulse pressure, ↓ urine output, normal BP

              Picture it: Bailing water out of a leaking boat keeps it level until the leak outpaces you.

              Where you'll see it
              • The compensatory stage
              • Why a normal BP doesn't rule out shock
              • Older adults on beta blockers may not get tachycardic
              C4

              Starving cells make lactate

              Without enough oxygen, cells switch to anaerobic metabolism, which makes little energy and a lot of lactic acid. Rising lactate and metabolic acidosis show tissues are starving.

              ↓ O₂ → anaerobic metabolism → ↑ lactate → metabolic acidosis

              Picture it: A car running on fumes sputters and leaves soot.

              Where you'll see it
              • Lactate in the sepsis bundle
              • Trending lactate to judge resuscitation
              • Acidosis worsens heart function
              C5

              Oxygen delivery depends on flow and content

              The oxygen reaching tissues depends on cardiac output and on how much oxygen the blood carries, which depends mostly on hemoglobin and saturation.

              DO₂ = CO × (1.34 × Hgb × SaO₂)

              Picture it: Deliveries depend on how many trucks run (flow) and how full each truck is (content).

              Where you'll see it
              • Transfusion in hemorrhagic shock
              • Oxygen therapy for low SpO₂
              • Inotropes in cardiogenic shock

              Shock type reasoning

              Pick the findings

              Worked example · read it step by step

              Mr. Samuel Ortiz, 67, is 2 days past a large anterior MI. He is restless. BP 84/56, HR 118, RR 26, SpO₂ 89%. Skin is cool and clammy. JVD at 45°, crackles halfway up both lungs, a new S3. Urine output was 15 mL in the last hour.

              1. Is this shock? Yes: hypotension plus signs of poor perfusion (restlessness, cool skin, low urine output).
              2. Check the tank. JVD and crackles mean preload is high. The tank isn't empty, so this isn't hypovolemic shock.
              3. Check the skin. Cool and clammy means the vessels are clamped down; early septic or anaphylactic shock would usually be warm.
              4. Find the cause. A large anterior MI damaged the pump. A full tank with a failing pump is cardiogenic shock.
              5. Decide the priority. Call for help, give oxygen, anticipate an inotrope such as dobutamine, reduce workload, and measure urine output hourly. Question a large fluid bolus, which would worsen the pulmonary edema.

              Sepsis and septic shock

              Sepsis is a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by the body's response to infection. Septic shock is sepsis with hypotension that needs vasopressors to keep the MAP at 65 or higher and a lactate over 2 mmol/L despite fluids.

              Screening: SIRS criteria (2 or more)

              • Temperature over 100.4°F (38°C) or under 96.8°F (36°C)
              • Heart rate over 90
              • Respiratory rate over 20 or PaCO₂ under 32
              • WBC over 12,000, under 4,000, or more than 10% bands

              Bedside red flags: qSOFA (2 or more)

              • Respiratory rate 22 or higher
              • Altered mental status
              • Systolic BP 100 or lower

              Older adults may have no fever, or may be hypothermic. New confusion can be the first sign.

              The hour-1 bundle

              StepWhy
              1. Measure lactateShows anaerobic metabolism; remeasure if over 2 mmol/L
              2. Blood cultures before antibioticsAntibiotics first can sterilize the cultures so the organism is never found

              Find and control the source (lines, catheters, wounds, abscesses), monitor MAP, urine output, mental status, lactate, and glucose, watch for fluid overload (crackles, falling SpO₂) during resuscitation, and watch for DIC.

              Section 7 · Shock

              Anaphylactic and neurogenic shock

              Both are distributive shock: the vessels dilate and blood pools in the periphery. The causes and the heart rate are very different.

              Anaphylactic

              Allergen triggers massive histamine release

              • Rapid onset after a trigger: drugs (antibiotics, contrast), foods, insect stings, latex
              • Hives, flushing, itching, angioedema
              • Stridor, hoarseness, wheezing, throat tightness
              • Hypotension, tachycardia; nausea, cramping
              • Epinephrine IM first (adults 0.3–0.5 mg in the anterolateral thigh; repeat every 5–15 min as needed)
              • Stop the trigger, airway, oxygen, fluids, position supine with legs raised (or sitting if breathing is hard)
              • Then antihistamines, bronchodilators, corticosteroids; observe for a biphasic reaction
              Neurogenic

              Loss of sympathetic tone

              • Spinal cord injury at T6 or above (also spinal anesthesia)
              • Hypotension with bradycardia (unopposed vagal tone)
              • Warm, dry skin below the injury; loss of temperature control
              • Spinal precautions
              • Fluids, vasopressors, atropine for bradycardia
              • Prevent hypothermia
              Don't confuse these

              Neurogenic shock (hemodynamic: low BP, slow HR, warm skin) is not the same as spinal shock (temporary loss of reflexes below the injury). A trauma client can have both, and can also be bleeding, so always look for hypovolemia too.

              Multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS)

              Prolonged hypoperfusion and inflammation damage organ after organ: lungs (ARDS), kidneys (acute kidney injury), liver (rising bilirubin and enzymes), blood (DIC), gut, and brain. The more organs that fail, the higher the mortality. Prevention means early recognition and fast restoration of perfusion.

                Key concepts

                These principles explain why the lab values and findings in the next sections happen. Learn them once and you can reason through conditions you haven't memorized.

                C1

                Oxygen content depends on hemoglobin

                SpO₂ tells you what percentage of the available hemoglobin is carrying oxygen. It does not tell you how much hemoglobin there is. Total oxygen delivered depends mostly on hemoglobin.

                O₂ content ≈ 1.34 × Hgb × SaO₂ (plus a small dissolved amount)

                Picture it: SpO₂ tells you how full each delivery truck is. Hemoglobin tells you how many trucks there are. Ten full trucks deliver less than thirty full trucks.

                Where you'll see it
                • Anemia: SpO₂ can read 98–100% while tissues are short of oxygen
                • Why transfusion, not more oxygen, fixes symptomatic anemia
                • Carbon monoxide poisoning can also give a falsely normal SpO₂
                C2

                The oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve

                Hemoglobin's grip on oxygen changes with conditions. A right shift loosens the grip and releases more oxygen to tissues. A left shift tightens the grip and releases less.

                Right shift: ↑ temp, ↑ CO₂, ↓ pH (acidosis), ↑ 2,3-DPG · Left shift: ↓ temp, ↓ CO₂, ↑ pH (alkalosis), fetal Hgb, stored blood

                Picture it: Think of exercising muscle: it's hot, acidic, and full of CO₂, so hemoglobin lets go of oxygen right where it's needed.

                Where you'll see it
                • Fever and acidosis increase oxygen release to tissues
                • Hypothermia and alkalosis make hemoglobin hold oxygen tighter
                • Fetal hemoglobin (raised by hydroxyurea in sickle cell disease) holds oxygen tightly and resists sickling
                • Stored blood is low in 2,3-DPG, so it releases oxygen less well at first
                C3

                Viscosity

                Thicker fluids flow more slowly. As hematocrit rises or plasma volume falls, blood becomes more viscous, flow slows, and clots form more easily.

                Picture it: Honey moves through a straw much more slowly than water.

                Where you'll see it
                • Polycythemia vera: thick blood causes headache, dizziness, and thrombosis
                • Dehydration triggers sickle cell crisis
                • Why hydration and phlebotomy are treatments
                C4

                Osmosis and tonicity

                Water moves across cell membranes toward the side with more dissolved particles. Red cells swell and burst in hypotonic fluid and shrink in hypertonic fluid.

                Isotonic (0.9% NaCl) · Hypotonic → cells swell · Hypertonic → cells shrink

                Picture it: A raisin left in water plumps up. Put a grape in salty water and it shrivels.

                Where you'll see it
                • Only 0.9% sodium chloride is used with blood products
                • D5W acts like a hypotonic fluid once the dextrose is used up, so it hemolyzes red cells
                • Hemolysis releases hemoglobin that can injure the kidneys
                C5

                Supply and demand

                Tissues get into trouble when oxygen demand exceeds oxygen supply. The body compensates first by raising heart rate and breathing rate.

                Picture it: A small delivery team can keep up on a slow day. On a busy day, they run faster, then fall behind.

                Where you'll see it
                • Anemia: fatigue and dyspnea with activity, tachycardia at rest when severe
                • Chest pain in severe anemia (the heart muscle is starved too)
                • Sickle cell pain: blocked vessels cut supply
                • Why clustering care and rest periods help
                C6

                Negative feedback

                The body senses a change and responds in a way that reverses it. Once the problem is corrected, the signal turns off.

                Low O₂ → kidneys release EPO → marrow makes RBCs → O₂ rises → EPO falls

                Picture it: A thermostat turns on the furnace when the room is cold and shuts it off once the room warms up.

                Where you'll see it
                • Chronic kidney disease: the kidneys can't send the EPO signal, so anemia develops
                • Secondary polycythemia: chronic hypoxia keeps EPO high
                • Polycythemia vera: the marrow ignores the signal, so EPO is low
                • Rising reticulocytes after blood loss
                C7

                Consumption: clotting is a balance

                Platelets and clotting factors are a limited supply. When clotting is switched on everywhere, they are used up faster than they are made, and bleeding follows.

                Clotting turned on everywhere → platelets and factors used up → bleeding

                Picture it: A bank account: if withdrawals outpace deposits, the balance hits zero even though money was there at the start.

                Where you'll see it
                • DIC: microclots consume platelets and factors, and then the client bleeds
                • TTP and HIT: platelets are used up in clots
                • Why DIC is treated by stopping the trigger and replacing what was consumed

                  Anemia and iron deficiency anemia

                  Anemia is a lower-than-normal hemoglobin or red cell count. It is a sign, not a diagnosis: something is causing it. The result is the same no matter the cause: less oxygen reaches the tissues.

                  Three ways to become anemic

                  MechanismExamplesReticulocyte count
                  Blood lossGI bleeding, heavy menstruation, trauma, surgeryRises (marrow responds)
                  Decreased productionIron, B12, or folate deficiency; aplastic anemia; chronic kidney disease (low EPO)Low
                  Increased destruction (hemolysis)Sickle cell disease, G6PD deficiency, autoimmune, transfusion reactionRises

                  What anemia looks like

                  Signs depend on how low and how fast. A slow drop allows the body to compensate; a fast drop does not.

                  • Mild: often no symptoms, or fatigue with exertion
                  • Moderate: fatigue, weakness, pallor, palpitations, dyspnea on exertion, headache
                  • Severe: tachycardia at rest, dyspnea at rest, dizziness, chest pain (the heart is starving too), cold intolerance, heart failure in older adults

                  Tachycardia is compensation: if each beat carries less oxygen, the heart beats faster to deliver the same amount.

                  Sorting anemia by cell size

                  The MCV (mean corpuscular volume, normal 80–100 fL) tells you the average size of the red cells. It is one of the fastest clues to the cause.

                  CBC interpreter

                  Try F, Hgb 9.2, MCV 72

                  Iron deficiency anemia

                  The most common anemia worldwide. Without enough iron, the marrow makes small, pale red cells: microcytic, hypochromic.

                  Causes: chronic blood loss (the most common cause in adults; GI bleeding must be ruled out, especially in men and postmenopausal women), heavy menstruation, pregnancy, poor intake, malabsorption (celiac disease, gastric bypass).

                  Findings

                  • General anemia signs: fatigue, pallor, dyspnea, tachycardia
                  • Pica: craving ice, clay, or starch
                  • Spoon-shaped nails (koilonychia)
                  • Smooth sore tongue (glossitis), cracks at the corners of the mouth (cheilitis)
                  • Labs: low Hgb, low MCV, low ferritin, low serum iron, high TIBC

                  Treatment

                  • Find and fix the cause of blood loss
                  • Iron-rich foods: red meat, organ meats, dark green leafy vegetables, legumes, fortified cereals
                  • Oral iron (ferrous sulfate)
                  • IV iron for malabsorption or intolerance; watch for hypersensitivity, especially with iron dextran (test dose)
                  Teaching: oral iron

                  Take on an empty stomach, about 1 hour before meals, if tolerated (with food if GI upset is a problem). Take with vitamin C or orange juice to increase absorption. Avoid taking with milk, antacids, tea, or coffee. Stools will turn dark green or black, which is expected. Constipation is common: increase fluids and fiber. Liquid iron stains teeth, so use a straw and rinse. Keep out of reach of children; iron overdose is dangerous.

                  Vitamin B12 and folate deficiency

                  Red cells need vitamin B12 and folic acid to make DNA. Without them, cells grow large but can't divide properly. The result is a macrocytic (megaloblastic) anemia: big, fragile red cells.

                  Vitamin B12 deficiency and pernicious anemia

                  B12 can only be absorbed in the ileum if it is bound to intrinsic factor, a protein made by the stomach's parietal cells. Pernicious anemia is an autoimmune disease that destroys those cells, so no intrinsic factor is made and dietary B12 passes through unabsorbed.

                  Other causes: gastrectomy or gastric bypass, ileal resection or Crohn's disease, strict vegan diet, long-term metformin or proton pump inhibitors, older age, chronic alcohol use.

                  Findings

                  • General anemia signs
                  • Smooth, beefy red, sore tongue
                  • Neurologic signs: numbness and tingling of hands and feet, loss of position sense, unsteady gait, memory loss, confusion
                  • Labs: low Hgb, high MCV, low serum B12; antibodies to intrinsic factor in pernicious anemia

                  Treatment

                  • Pernicious anemia and malabsorption: lifelong B12 injections (IM or deep SubQ cyanocobalamin), usually weekly then monthly; high-dose oral or nasal forms are options for some
                  • Dietary cause: oral B12 and diet (meat, eggs, dairy, fortified foods)
                  • Neurologic damage may be permanent if treatment is delayed

                  Folate deficiency

                  Causes

                  • Poor diet (few fresh vegetables)
                  • Chronic alcohol use (most common in adults)
                  • Increased need: pregnancy, hemolytic anemia
                  • Malabsorption
                  • Drugs: methotrexate, phenytoin, trimethoprim

                  Findings and treatment

                  • Same macrocytic anemia as B12 deficiency
                  • No neurologic signs
                  • Low serum folate
                  • Oral folic acid; leafy greens, citrus, beans, fortified grains
                  • Folic acid before and during pregnancy prevents neural tube defects
                  Why it matters which one

                  Giving folic acid to someone who is actually B12 deficient will improve the blood counts but will not stop the nerve damage. The anemia looks fixed while the neurologic injury keeps progressing. B12 levels are checked before folate is given alone.

                  Hemolytic and aplastic anemia

                  These two are opposites. In hemolytic anemia the marrow works hard, but red cells are destroyed too early. In aplastic anemia the marrow itself fails.

                  Hemolytic anemia

                  Red cells are destroyed before their 120-day life span ends. The marrow tries to keep up, so reticulocytes rise.

                  Causes

                  • Inherited: sickle cell disease, G6PD deficiency (hemolysis triggered by certain drugs, infections, fava beans), hereditary spherocytosis, thalassemia
                  • Acquired: autoimmune hemolytic anemia, transfusion reaction, mechanical heart valves, infections (malaria), some drugs

                  Findings

                  • Anemia signs
                  • Jaundice and dark urine (hemoglobin breakdown)
                  • Splenomegaly (spleen working overtime)
                  • Gallstones (pigment stones) over time
                  • Labs: high reticulocytes, high indirect (unconjugated) bilirubin, high LDH, low haptoglobin

                  Treatment: remove the cause (stop the drug, treat infection), corticosteroids for autoimmune types, folic acid (the busy marrow uses more), transfusion if severe, splenectomy in some cases. Keep the client hydrated to protect the kidneys from hemoglobin breakdown products.

                  Aplastic anemia

                  The bone marrow stops producing cells. Because the stem cell is affected, all three lines drop: pancytopenia (low RBCs, WBCs, and platelets).

                  Causes: often idiopathic or autoimmune; chemotherapy and radiation; certain drugs (chloramphenicol, some anticonvulsants); toxins such as benzene and pesticides; viral infections (hepatitis, Epstein-Barr).

                  Sickle cell disease

                  An inherited disorder in which abnormal hemoglobin (HbS) makes red cells stiff and crescent-shaped when oxygen is low. Sickled cells jam small vessels, starve tissue of oxygen, and are destroyed early.

                  Inheritance

                  Sickle cell trait (HbAS)

                  • One sickle gene, one normal gene
                  • Usually no symptoms
                  • Can pass the gene to children
                  • Possible sickling with extreme dehydration, exertion, or high altitude

                  Sickle cell disease (HbSS)

                  • Two sickle genes (autosomal recessive)
                  • Two carrier parents: 25% chance of disease with each pregnancy
                  • Most common in people of African, Mediterranean, Middle Eastern, and South Asian descent
                  • Detected on newborn screening

                  The sickling cycle

                  Trigger → low O₂ → HbS stiffens → cells sickle → cells clump and block small vessels → tissue hypoxia and pain → more sickling

                  Triggers: dehydration, infection and fever, hypoxia, cold exposure, high altitude, strenuous exercise, stress, alcohol, smoking.

                  Priorities

                  Polycythemia

                  The opposite of anemia: too many red cells. The blood becomes thick (hyperviscous), flows slowly, and clots easily.

                  Primary

                  Polycythemia vera

                  • A myeloproliferative bone marrow disorder, usually from a JAK2 gene mutation
                  • Marrow overproduces RBCs, and often WBCs and platelets too
                  • Erythropoietin is low (the body is trying to slow production)
                  • Usually adults over 60
                  Secondary

                  Secondary polycythemia

                  • A response to chronic low oxygen
                  • COPD, smoking, sleep apnea, high altitude, some congenital heart disease
                  • Also EPO-secreting tumors, testosterone use
                  • Erythropoietin is high; treat the cause

                  Findings of hyperviscosity

                  • Ruddy, flushed (plethoric) face and dusky red mucous membranes
                  • Headache, dizziness, vision changes, tinnitus, fatigue
                  • Hypertension
                  • Itching that worsens after a warm bath or shower (histamine release from extra basophils)
                  • Burning pain and redness in hands and feet (erythromelalgia)
                  • Splenomegaly
                  • Thrombosis: DVT, PE, stroke, MI; this is the main cause of death
                  • Paradoxically, bleeding can occur too (abnormal platelet function)
                  • Labs: Hgb and Hct high (Hct often above 55%), often high WBCs and platelets

                  Treatment and nursing care

                  • Therapeutic phlebotomy: removing blood (often about 500 mL at a time) to keep Hct below 45%. Monitor for dizziness and hypotension afterward.
                  • Low-dose aspirin to reduce clot risk
                  • Hydroxyurea or other marrow suppressants for high-risk clients; ruxolitinib (JAK inhibitor) in some cases
                  • Encourage fluids (2–3 L/day unless restricted) and regular walking; avoid sitting for long periods and tight clothing
                  • Avoid iron supplements (iron fuels more RBC production)
                  • Lukewarm or cool bathing, pat skin dry, antihistamines for itching
                  • Teach signs of clots: leg pain and swelling, chest pain, sudden dyspnea, stroke symptoms

                  The basics

                  • Labs: prolonged aPTT; normal PT and platelet count

                  Findings

                  • Hemarthrosis: bleeding into joints (knees, ankles, elbows), causing pain, swelling, and over time permanent joint damage
                  • Deep muscle hematomas
                  • Prolonged bleeding after dental work, circumcision, or surgery; bleeding can start hours later
                  • Intracranial hemorrhage after head injury: the most dangerous complication
                  • Factor replacement (factor VIII or IX concentrate), either on a regular prophylactic schedule or at the first sign of a bleed. Clients and families learn to infuse at home.
                  • Desmopressin (DDAVP) releases stored factor VIII and von Willebrand factor; useful for mild hemophilia A (not B).
                  • Emicizumab: a SubQ antibody that mimics factor VIII, used for prophylaxis in hemophilia A.
                  • Joint bleed: give factor first, then rest, ice, compression, and elevation; immobilize briefly, then physical therapy.
                  • Acetaminophen for pain; avoid aspirin and NSAIDs. Avoid IM injections. Medical alert ID. Noncontact sports such as swimming are encouraged.

                  Von Willebrand disease

                  The most common inherited bleeding disorder. It affects males and females equally (usually autosomal dominant). Von Willebrand factor (vWF) does two jobs: it is the glue that lets platelets stick to the injured vessel wall, and it carries and protects factor VIII.

                  • Findings: mostly mucosal bleeding: frequent nosebleeds, bleeding gums, heavy menstrual periods, easy bruising, bleeding after dental work
                  • Labs: low vWF activity and antigen; aPTT may be normal or prolonged; platelet count normal
                  • Treatment: desmopressin (most type 1), vWF-containing factor concentrates, antifibrinolytics (tranexamic acid) for mucosal or dental bleeding, hormonal contraceptives for heavy menses

                  Platelet problem or clotting factor problem?

                  The pattern

                  Platelet problems (thrombocytopenia, von Willebrand disease) bleed right away and from the surface: skin, gums, nose, petechiae. Clotting factor problems (hemophilia) bleed deep and often late: joints, muscles, hours after a procedure.

                  Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)

                  DIC is never a disease on its own. It is a complication of another serious illness in which the clotting system turns on throughout the body. Clients clot and bleed at the same time.

                  How DIC develops

                  1. A serious trigger releases clotting activators into the bloodstream.
                  2. Tiny clots form throughout small vessels, blocking flow to organs (clotting phase).
                  3. Platelets and clotting factors are used up faster than they can be made.
                  4. Fibrinolysis breaks down the clots, releasing fibrin degradation products, which also act as anticoagulants.
                  5. With no platelets or factors left, the client bleeds from everywhere (bleeding phase).

                  Triggers

                  Sepsis (most common), major trauma, burns, crush injuries, shock, cancer (especially acute leukemia), obstetric complications (placental abruption, amniotic fluid embolism, retained fetal tissue, HELLP syndrome), acute hemolytic transfusion reaction, and snake venom.

                  Findings

                  Bleeding
                  • Oozing from IV sites, incisions, and puncture sites
                  • Petechiae, purpura, ecchymoses
                  • Bleeding gums, epistaxis
                  • Hematuria, GI bleeding (black stools, coffee-ground emesis)
                  • Signs of internal bleeding: tachycardia, falling BP
                  Clotting
                  • Cool, mottled, or cyanotic fingers and toes (acrocyanosis)
                  • Kidneys: oliguria, rising creatinine
                  • Lungs: dyspnea, hypoxemia
                  • Brain: confusion, decreased LOC
                  • Skin and digital necrosis

                  Treatment and nursing care

                  Treat the cause first

                  DIC will not stop until the trigger is controlled: antibiotics and source control for sepsis, delivery for obstetric causes, fluids and pressors for shock.

                  • Replace what was consumed: platelets, fresh frozen plasma (clotting factors), cryoprecipitate (fibrinogen), and PRBCs for blood loss.
                  • Heparin is sometimes used when clotting dominates (for example, organ ischemia), to stop the consumption cycle.
                  • Bleeding precautions; minimize needle sticks; hold pressure longer; avoid IM injections.
                  • Monitor vitals, urine output, neuro status, SpO₂, and skin color and temperature of extremities; trend labs.
                  • Gentle oral care, no rectal measures; measure blood loss (weigh dressings, test stool and urine).

                  Blood transfusion

                  Many hematologic disorders are supported with blood products. Transfusion is common, but a single identification error can be fatal, so every step has a reason.

                  Blood products

                  ProductUsed forNotes
                  Packed red blood cells (PRBCs)Symptomatic anemia, blood loss1 unit raises Hgb about 1 g/dL and Hct about 3%; infuse within 4 hours
                  PlateletsLow platelets with bleeding or before proceduresInfuse quickly (usually 15–60 min) through a standard blood set; never refrigerate
                  Fresh frozen plasma (FFP)Replacing clotting factors (DIC, liver failure, warfarin reversal with bleeding)Must be ABO compatible
                  CryoprecipitateLow fibrinogen (DIC, massive bleeding)Rich in fibrinogen, factor VIII, vWF, factor XIII

                  Compatibility

                  Red cells

                  • O negative: universal red cell donor (no A, B, or Rh antigens)
                  • AB positive: universal red cell recipient
                  • Rh-negative clients should receive Rh-negative blood

                  Plasma

                  • Reversed: AB plasma is the universal plasma donor (no anti-A or anti-B antibodies)
                  • Type O plasma can only go to type O recipients

                  Safe administration

                  1. Verify the provider's order and the client's signed consent. Confirm type and crossmatch is complete.
                  2. Establish IV access: 18- or 20-gauge in adults (smaller is acceptable when needed, per policy).
                  3. Prime the blood administration set (with in-line filter) with 0.9% sodium chloride only. Never add medications or use dextrose solutions, which cause hemolysis.
                  4. Take baseline vital signs, including temperature.
                  5. Two licensed nurses verify at the bedside: client name and second identifier, blood band number, ABO and Rh, unit number, expiration, and product.
                  6. Start slowly and stay with the client for the first 15 minutes, when most severe reactions happen. Repeat vital signs per policy (commonly at 15 minutes and then periodically).
                  7. Complete each unit of PRBCs within 4 hours of leaving the blood bank.

Treat as ordered (antipyretics, antihistamines, diuretics, epinephrine for anaphylaxis) and document.
