The Spark Line

Every bump on an ECG is a story about charge moving through heart muscle. Watch one beat in slow motion, then test how the direction of that charge decides whether the pen goes up, down, or both.

Part 1 · Vocabulary

Words to own

DepolarizationThe inside of the cell flips from negative to positive as ions (Na⁺ or Ca²⁺) rush in. An electrical event, and the trigger for contraction.
RepolarizationThe cell restores its negative interior, mainly by letting K⁺ flow out. The cell resets so it can fire again.
AutomaticityPacemaker cells (SA node first) drift toward threshold on their own and fire without an outside signal.
Isoelectric lineThe flat baseline. No net current is moving toward or away from the electrode, so the pen stays level.
DeflectionAny movement of the tracing off baseline. Upward is positive, downward is negative, both is biphasic.
Refractory periodThe window when a cell cannot respond (absolute) or needs a stronger-than-normal stimulus (relative) to fire again.

Part 3 · Deflection lab

Why the pen goes up or down

An electrode is a one-way eye. Move the positive electrode around a strip of muscle and watch what it records as a wave crosses the strip.

What the electrode records

Two negatives make an upright T wave.
Repolarization moving toward a positive electrode draws a downward line. But the ventricles repolarize in reverse, from the outer wall (epicardium) back toward the inner wall (endocardium), because outer cells have shorter action potentials. A reversed wave of a reversed charge points the pen up again, so in Lead II the T wave is upright, matching the QRS. Try it: pick Repolarization, then Away.
Lead II in one sentence.
Its positive electrode sits at the lower left, near the apex. Normal conduction runs from the SA node (upper right) down toward the apex, straight at that electrode, which is why a healthy Lead II shows an upright P, a tall R, and an upright T.
