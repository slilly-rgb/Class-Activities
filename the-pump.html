The Pump

How the heart is built, how blood moves through it, and the words we use to describe how well it works.

Open (2 min). Ask: "How many times has your heart beaten since you sat down?" At about 70 bpm, that's roughly 100,000 beats a day and 2.5 billion in a lifetime.

Today's goal is the working picture a nurse needs: where the heart sits, how it's built, the path blood takes, what happens in one beat, and the vocabulary for how well it pumps (CO, SV, EF, preload, afterload, contractility).

Not covered today: electrical conduction and ECG interpretation in depth.

01 · Location

Where the heart sits

  • In the mediastinum, between the lungs, behind the sternum. About two-thirds lies left of midline.
  • About the size of the patient's closed fist, roughly 250–350 g.
  • The base is the top, where the great vessels attach (about the 2nd intercostal space).
  • The apex points down and to the left. It taps the chest wall at the 5th ICS, left midclavicular line: the PMI (point of maximal impulse).

PMI shifted down and to the left? Think of an enlarged heart, as in heart failure.

Click through the four facts. Orientation reminder: the drawing faces the class, so the patient's left is on the screen's right.

Try it: have students find the 5th ICS on themselves (angle of Louis = 2nd rib, count down) and palpate their own PMI, or listen there.

Clinical tie: a PMI that is displaced laterally or feels broad suggests cardiomegaly (HF, long-standing HTN). Harder to feel with obesity or COPD.

02 · Layers

Outside in: the sac and the wall

Layer

Select a layer to describe it.

Click each band from the outside in. Mnemonic for the wall: Epi-Myo-Endo (on, muscle, inside).

  • Pericardial cavity: tie to tamponade. Fluid collects faster than the fibrous sac can stretch, so the heart can't fill. Beck's triad: hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds.
  • Pericarditis: friction rub, sharp chest pain better when leaning forward.
  • Myocardium: the pump. MI kills this layer and lowers contractility.
  • Endocardium: continuous with the valves; endocarditis threatens the valves (IV drug use, prosthetic valves, dental procedures).

Click through the four phases, then press Play heartbeat (about 2.5× slower than a real beat at 75 bpm). Sound plays only from the computer where you turn it on.

  • Diastole takes about two-thirds of each beat at a normal rate. The atrial kick adds roughly 20–30% of ventricular filling, which is lost in atrial fibrillation.
  • S1 = AV valves closing = start of systole. Loudest at the apex. Feel the carotid pulse while listening: S1 lines up with it.
  • S2 = semilunar valves closing = start of diastole. Loudest at the base.

Ask: "When both sets of valves are closed, what is the ventricle doing?" (Building or dropping pressure with no volume change: isovolumetric contraction and relaxation.)

06 · Coronary circulation

The pump feeds itself first

The coronary arteries branch off the aorta just above the aortic valve.

  • Right coronary artery (RCA): right atrium and ventricle, inferior wall of the LV. Supplies the SA node in about 60% of people and the AV node in most.
  • Left anterior descending (LAD): front of the LV and most of the septum. A blockage here causes a large anterior MI.
  • Circumflex: lateral and back walls of the LV. The left main artery splits into the LAD and circumflex.
Key point

Coronary arteries fill during diastole

When the ventricle contracts, it squeezes its own arteries shut. Faster heart rates shorten diastole, so the heart gets less blood flow just as it needs more oxygen.

Drag the heart-rate slider from 60 to 150. Point out that systole barely shrinks while diastole collapses. That's why tachycardia can trigger angina in a patient with CAD, and why rate control (e.g., beta blockers) helps.

07 · Cardiac output

How much does it pump?

SV = EDV − ESV  ·  EF = SV ÷ EDV  ·  CO = HR × SV

Stroke volumeBlood ejected per beat · normal 60–100 mL
Ejection fractionShare of the filled ventricle ejected · normal 55–70%
Cardiac outputBlood pumped per minute · normal 4–8 L/min

Have students predict before you click each preset.

  • Dehydrated: low filling (EDV) → low SV. HR rises to hold CO near normal. Tachycardia is often the first sign of fluid loss.
  • Weak pump (HFrEF): the ventricle is dilated and full, but ejects a small share. EF about 29%. CO can look acceptable at rest because HR and dilation compensate.
  • Athlete: large SV lets a slow HR deliver normal CO.

Key formula to memorize: CO = HR × SV. Anything that lowers HR or SV lowers CO unless the other compensates.

08 · What sets stroke volume

Preload, afterload, contractility

Frank–Starling law: the more the muscle is stretched during filling, the harder it contracts, up to a limit. Overstretch it, like an old rubber band, and the contraction weakens. That's part of what happens in heart failure.

Three things set stroke volume. Use the analogies: balloon (preload), heavy door (afterload), strength of the squeeze (contractility). Reveal each card's details after the class guesses what raises and lowers it.

Connect to assessment: preload shows up as JVD, edema and crackles when high; flat neck veins, hypotension and low urine output when low. Afterload tracks with blood pressure and SVR.

Drug vocabulary: positive inotropes raise contractility; vasodilators lower afterload; diuretics and nitrates lower preload.
