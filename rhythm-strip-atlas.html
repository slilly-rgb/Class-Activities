Where the leads come from

A lead is a point of view: a negative and a positive electrode looking at the heart's electricity from one angle. The idea began with a Dutch physiologist, a thread finer than a hair, and three buckets of salt water.

1887The British physiologist Augustus Waller recorded the first human heartbeat from the skin. His instrument, a capillary electrometer, made faint, blurred tracings that were hard to measure.

1901Willem Einthoven, a physiologist at Leiden University in the Netherlands, wanted a cleaner signal. He built the string galvanometer: a silver-coated quartz thread stretched between powerful electromagnets. The heart's tiny current made the thread twitch, and its shadow was projected onto moving photographic film.

The machine weighed about 600 pounds, needed water to cool its magnets, and took five people to run. Patients sat with both arms and the left leg in buckets of salt water, which served as the electrodes.

1905Einthoven named the deflections P, Q, R, S and T, the letters we still use. He even sent tracings from the hospital to his lab about 1.5 km away over telephone wires, the first "telecardiogram." In 1924 he received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Einthoven's triangle

The three limb leads

  • Lead I: right arm (−) to left arm (+)
  • Lead II: right arm (−) to left leg (+)
  • Lead III: left arm (−) to left leg (+)
  • Einthoven's law: the voltage in lead I plus lead III equals lead II

Why the picture changes by lead

Electricity moving toward a positive electrode writes an upward deflection; moving away writes a downward one. The heart's impulse travels from the SA node (upper right) toward the apex (lower left), which lines up almost exactly with lead II. That is why lead II shows the tallest, clearest upright P waves and QRS complexes.

From 3 leads to 12

In the 1930s and 1940s Frank Wilson added the chest (precordial) leads and Emanuel Goldberger added the augmented limb leads (aVR, aVL, aVF). Together with Einthoven's three, they make the 12-lead ECG used to locate ischemia and infarction.

At the bedside

A 3-lead monitor uses white on the right shoulder, black on the left shoulder, and red low on the left side ("white on right, smoke over fire"). A 5-lead adds green low on the right and brown on the chest. Most monitors display lead II by default.

QT shortens as heart rate rises, so compare the corrected QT (QTc). A QRS of 0.12 s or more is called wide and means the ventricles were not activated through the normal fast pathway.

P wave atrial depolarization

Small, rounded, upright in lead II. One P before every QRS means the SA node is in charge.

PR segment AV node delay

The flat line after the P. The AV node holds the impulse about 0.1 s so the atria can finish emptying into the ventricles.

QRS complex ventricular depolarization

Tall and fast because the His–Purkinje system spreads the impulse almost instantly. Atrial repolarization is hidden inside it.

T wave ventricular repolarization

The ventricles reset. During the second half of the T wave the heart is vulnerable, which is why an early beat landing there (R-on-T) can trigger VF.

The 6-second strip

Thirty large boxes equal six seconds. Count the QRS complexes inside that window and multiply by 10 to get beats per minute. This is the method you will use for every strip in this course. It works on any rhythm, including irregular ones.

300 method

300 ÷ large boxes R to R

1500 method

1500 ÷ small boxes R to R

Most precise for regular rhythms. 1,500 small boxes pass in one minute at 25 mm/s.

    Electrical therapy · 1 of 4

    Pace, cardiovert or defibrillate?

    Two questions decide which electrical therapy a rhythm gets: is the heart too slow or too fast, and does the patient have a pulse?

    Too slow, with a pulse

    Pacing

    For bradyarrhythmias causing symptoms: symptomatic sinus bradycardia, second-degree type II and third-degree AV block, slow idioventricular rhythm with a pulse.

    Small, repeated impulses make the ventricles depolarize at a rate you set.

    Medications: atropine first; dopamine or epinephrine infusion; analgesia and sedation.

    Too fast, with a pulse

    Synchronized cardioversion

    For tachyarrhythmias making the patient unstable: SVT and atrial tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, VT with a pulse.

    A shock timed to the R wave resets the rhythm without landing on the T wave.

    Medications: sedation and analgesia; antiarrhythmics; anticoagulation for AF or flutter.

    No pulse, shockable

    Defibrillation

    For pulseless VT and VF only.

    An unsynchronized high-energy shock depolarizes the whole heart at once, stopping the chaos so the SA node can take over.

    Medications: epinephrine; amiodarone or lidocaine.

    ✕

    Never shock PEA or asystoleThey are pulseless but not shockable. Give high-quality CPR and epinephrine, and find the cause (H's and T's).

    Electrical therapy · 2 of 4

    Transcutaneous pacing

    When the heart's own pacemaker is too slow or blocked, an external pacemaker sends an impulse through pads on the chest and the ventricles follow it.

    Electrical capture · paced at 60/minSpike → wide QRS → T, every time
    Failure to capture · current too low
    Top: every pacer spike is followed by a wide QRS. Bottom: spikes land with nothing after them while the patient's own slow beats continue. Turn up the current (mA).

    Steps

    1. Assess the patient. Is the bradycardia causing hypotension, altered mental status, chest pain, signs of shock or acute heart failure?
    2. Give atropine while you set up, as ordered. With second-degree type II or third-degree block it may not work; do not delay pacing.
    3. Explain what will happen, and give analgesia and sedation as ordered. Pacing hurts.
    4. Apply pads anterior–posterior (left chest and left back) or anterior–lateral. Keep the ECG leads on: the pacer needs them to sense the heart.
    5. Set demand mode and a rate, usually 60–80/min as ordered.
    6. Increase the current (mA) until every spike is followed by a wide QRS and T wave: electrical capture.
    7. Confirm mechanical capture: a femoral pulse matching the paced rate, and a blood pressure. Muscle twitching can feel like a carotid pulse.
    8. Set the current slightly above the capture threshold, per the device.
    9. Check capture, pain and the skin under the pads at every assessment; prepare for transvenous pacing.

    Electrical therapy · 3 of 4

    Synchronized cardioversion

    A shock timed to land on the R wave, used when a fast rhythm with a pulse is making the patient unstable.

    SYNC on · tachycardia at 160/min▼ sync marker on each R wave · ■ vulnerable T wave
    A shock that lands on the T wave can throw the heart into VF. With SYNC on, the defibrillator waits and fires on the next R wave.

    Steps

    1. Assess the patient. Pulse present? Unstable: hypotension, acutely altered mental status, signs of shock, ischemic chest pain, acute heart failure?
    2. Call for help. Have oxygen, IV access, suction and airway equipment ready.
    3. Give sedation and analgesia as ordered; get consent if time allows.
    4. Apply pads and ECG leads.
    5. Press SYNC. Confirm a marker appears on every R wave, not on T waves.
    6. Select the energy ordered.
    7. Charge. "I'm clear, you're clear, everyone's clear." Oxygen away from the chest.
    8. Press and hold the shock button until it fires; there may be a short pause while it waits for an R wave.
    9. Reassess rhythm and pulse. Before another shock, turn SYNC back on: many devices switch it off after each shock.
    10. If the patient becomes pulseless in VF, turn SYNC off and defibrillate.

    Electrical therapy · 4 of 4 Cardiac arrest

    Defibrillation: pulseless VT and VF

    The two shockable arrest rhythms. Survival falls with every minute without CPR and a shock, so the first minutes follow a fixed sequence.

    Ventricular fibrillationNo R waves: nothing to synchronize to
    Defibrillation is unsynchronized. The shock depolarizes the whole myocardium at once, stopping the chaotic activity so the SA node can take back control.
