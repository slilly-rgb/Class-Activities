1 · Refresh about 5 min

Blood flow through the heart

Vena cavae→Right atrium→Tricuspid valve→Right ventricle→Pulmonic valve→Pulmonary artery→Lungs→Pulmonary veins→Left atrium→Mitral valve→Left ventricle→Aortic valve→Aorta

Blue: deoxygenated blood on the right side. Red: oxygenated blood on the left side.

Cardiac output and its four levers

CO = HR × SV

Cardiac output is the liters of blood the heart pumps each minute (normal about 4–8 L/min). Stroke volume is set by preload, afterload and contractility.

PreloadHow much the ventricle is stretched at the end of filling. More volume means more stretch.
A rubber band pulled back further snaps harder, up to a point.
AfterloadThe resistance the ventricle pushes against to eject blood. High blood pressure and a narrow aortic valve raise it.
Pushing open a heavy door takes more work.
ContractilityHow forcefully the muscle squeezes, regardless of stretch. Damaged muscle squeezes less.
A strong hand vs. a tired hand squeezing the same ball.
Heart rateFaster raises output only to a point. A very fast rate cuts filling time, so stroke volume falls.
Refilling a bucket too quickly leaves it half full.

2 · Assessment, labs and diagnostics about 10 min

Where to listen

S1 ("lub") is the mitral and tricuspid valves closing at the start of systole. It is loudest at the apex.

S2 ("dub") is the aortic and pulmonic valves closing at the start of diastole. It is loudest at the base.

S3 comes just after S2, in early diastole, as blood rushes into a full or failing ventricle. In adults it usually points to fluid overload or heart failure.

S4 comes just before S1, in late diastole, as the atria push blood into a stiff ventricle (hypertension, ischemia).

Worked example · read it step by step

Mr. Leon Price, 58, came to the ED with "indigestion" and unusual fatigue since yesterday. He vomited three times overnight. Here are his labs:

Lab0 hr3 hr6 hrRange
hs-Troponin I (ng/L)1296 H284 H≤ 20
Potassium (mEq/L)3.3 L——3.5–5.0
Magnesium (mEq/L)1.9——1.5–2.5

How an experienced nurse reads this:

  1. Compare each value to the range. The first troponin is normal, but it was drawn early. A normal early troponin does not rule out an MI.
  2. Read the trend, not one value. Troponin rises from 12 to 96 to 284. A rising pattern across serial draws means heart muscle is being injured right now.
  3. Connect it to the person. "Indigestion" and fatigue are atypical MI symptoms. They are common in women, older adults and people with diabetes, so don't wait for classic chest pain.
  4. Look for what makes it more dangerous. Potassium 3.3 after vomiting raises the risk of ventricular dysrhythmias in ischemic heart muscle. Magnesium is normal.
  5. Decide the nursing priority. Report the troponin trend and the low potassium now, keep him on continuous cardiac monitoring, keep him on rest to lower oxygen demand, and anticipate potassium replacement by IV infusion pump or by mouth, never IV push.
