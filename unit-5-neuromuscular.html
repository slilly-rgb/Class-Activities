Neuromuscular

The nervous system senses, decides, and moves the body; the musculoskeletal system carries out the movement; the eyes and ears bring in the world. This unit follows that system from the neuron to the whole person: brain and spinal cord conditions, conditions in children, thinking and memory, mobility, and sensory perception.

The unit has seven sections. Each one has an introduction, terminology, foundations and key concepts, condition pages with quick checks, a section test, a list of what to bring to class, and a Ticket to Class. Start with Section 1: every other section uses its neuro assessment and concepts.

Section 1 · Neurological Foundations

The body's control system

Every neurological condition in this unit is a normal process breaking down: a signal that can't travel, a region that loses its blood supply, or pressure that has nowhere to go. Learn the normal first and the abnormal makes sense.

You will use this section in every other section of Unit 5. The neuro assessment you learn here (level of consciousness, Glasgow Coma Scale, pupils, motor and sensory checks) is how you will catch a rising ICP, an extending stroke, or a spinal cord injury that is getting worse.

Keep three questions in mind: Where in the nervous system is the problem? Is the brain getting enough oxygen and glucose? Is the client better, worse, or the same as the last assessment?

Learning objectives

When you finish, you should be able to:

  1. Define the medical terms used to describe neurological structure, function, and assessment.
  2. Describe how a neuron conducts an impulse and passes it across a synapse.
  3. Identify the major regions of the brain, the cranial nerves, and the functions of each.
  4. Explain how CSF, the blood-brain barrier, and the Monro-Kellie doctrine protect the brain, and calculate cerebral perfusion pressure.
  5. Apply core principles (brain fuel, the fixed box, perfusion pressure, LOC first, location equals function, transmitter balance, upper vs. lower motor neuron) to neurological findings.
  6. Perform and interpret a focused neurological assessment, including the Glasgow Coma Scale and pupil checks.
  7. Describe nursing care before and after common neurological diagnostic tests.

Section 1 · Neurological Foundations

Neurons and the brain

The nervous system is built from one kind of signaling cell, the neuron, wired into a central system (brain and spinal cord) and a peripheral system (cranial and spinal nerves).

How a neuron signals

  • Dendrites receive signals; the cell body processes them; the axon carries the impulse away.
  • An impulse is an action potential: sodium rushes in (depolarization), then potassium moves out (repolarization).
  • Myelin, made by Schwann cells (peripheral) and oligodendrocytes (central), insulates the axon so the impulse jumps between gaps (nodes of Ranvier). Loss of myelin slows or blocks signals, as in multiple sclerosis and Guillain-Barré syndrome.
  • At the synapse, the impulse releases a neurotransmitter that crosses the gap and binds receptors on the next cell.

Crossed wiring

Motor and sensory pathways cross to the other side in the brainstem. A lesion in the left hemisphere causes weakness and numbness on the right side of the body. The cerebellum is the exception: it affects coordination on the same side.

Why this matters at the bedside

A dilating, sluggish pupil (CN III) can be the first sign that a swelling brain is pressing on the brainstem. A weak gag and cough (IX and X) after a stroke mean aspiration risk: keep the client NPO until a swallow screen is passed.

The autonomic nervous system

Sympathetic ("fight or flight")

  • Neurotransmitter: norepinephrine
  • ↑ HR and BP, dilated pupils, bronchodilation
  • Slows digestion and bladder emptying
  • Leaves the cord at T1–L2

Parasympathetic ("rest and digest")

  • Neurotransmitter: acetylcholine
  • ↓ HR, constricted pupils, more secretions
  • Stimulates digestion, urination
  • Carried mainly by the vagus nerve and sacral nerves

Spinal cord injury at T6 or above cuts the brain off from the sympathetic outflow. That is why these clients develop neurogenic shock early and autonomic dysreflexia later (Section 5).

Section 1 · Neurological Foundations

CSF, the blood-brain barrier, and intracranial pressure

The brain sits in a rigid box. Anything that adds volume inside that box raises the pressure, and rising pressure squeezes blood out of the brain.

Protection

  • The skull, the three meninges (dura mater, arachnoid, pia mater), and cerebrospinal fluid cushion the brain.
  • CSF is made in the choroid plexus of the ventricles (about 500 mL a day), circulates around the brain and cord, and is absorbed by the arachnoid villi. Normal CSF is clear, with glucose about 60% of blood glucose and very few cells.
  • The blood-brain barrier is made of tight junctions in brain capillaries. It keeps toxins out but also blocks many drugs. Inflammation and injury make it leaky, which adds to brain swelling.

The Monro-Kellie doctrine

The skull holds three things: brain tissue (about 80%), blood (about 10%), and CSF (about 10%). If one increases, another must decrease or the pressure rises. Early on, CSF is pushed into the spinal canal and venous blood leaves the skull. When those buffers are used up, a small added volume causes a large jump in pressure.

Section 1 · Neurological Foundations

Key concepts

These principles explain why neurological findings happen. Learn them once and you can reason through conditions you haven't memorized.

C1

The brain can't store fuel

Neurons need a constant supply of oxygen and glucose. Within minutes of losing either, they stop working; within a few more, they die.

Low O₂, low glucose, or low blood flow → confusion first → loss of consciousness → cell death

Picture it: A city with no power plant reserve: the moment the grid fails, the lights go out.

Where you'll see it
  • Hypoglycemia and hypoxia cause confusion before anything else
  • Stroke: 'time is brain'
  • Check a blood glucose in any client with a sudden change in LOC
C2

The skull is a fixed box

The skull can't expand. Brain, blood, and CSF share a fixed space; if one grows, the others must shrink or the pressure rises (Monro-Kellie).

Brain + blood + CSF = constant volume

Picture it: A packed suitcase: once it's full, adding one more shirt strains the zipper.

Where you'll see it
  • Bleeding, swelling, tumors, and hydrocephalus all raise ICP
  • Infants' open fontanels let the head grow, delaying signs
  • Head of bed 30° and head midline help venous blood drain
C3

Perfusion needs pressure

Blood reaches the brain only when MAP is high enough to overcome ICP. As ICP rises or BP falls, brain perfusion drops.

CPP = MAP − ICP (goal 60–70 mm Hg or more)

Picture it: Watering a garden uphill: the steeper the hill, the more pressure the hose needs.

Where you'll see it
  • Avoid hypotension after head injury
  • Cushing's response raises BP to keep the brain perfused
  • Treat high ICP and low MAP together
C4

LOC changes first

The cerebral cortex is the most sensitive part of the brain, so a change in level of consciousness is the earliest sign of neurological decline. Vital sign changes come late.

Earliest: restlessness, confusion → Later: pupil changes → Last: Cushing's triad

Picture it: The canary in the coal mine stops singing before the miners feel anything.

Where you'll see it
  • Report new restlessness or confusion right away
  • Compare every neuro check with the last
  • Don't wait for vital signs to change
C5

Location equals function

Neurological signs depend on where the damage is, not what caused it. Motor and sensory pathways cross, so brain lesions affect the opposite side of the body.

Left hemisphere lesion → right-sided weakness (+ aphasia if language areas involved)

Picture it: A blown fuse: what goes dark depends on which circuit the fuse controls, not why it blew.

Where you'll see it
  • Stroke, tumor, and TBI can all cause the same deficit
  • Spinal cord injury level predicts function
  • Cerebellar lesions affect the same side
C6

Transmitters work in balance

Normal function depends on a balance of excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmitters. Too much excitation causes seizures; too little of one transmitter causes predictable deficits.

Dopamine ↓ vs. acetylcholine → Parkinson's tremor and rigidity · GABA ↓ → seizures

Picture it: A seesaw: take weight off one side and the other side drops.

Where you'll see it
  • Parkinson's: low dopamine
  • Myasthenia gravis: acetylcholine can't reach its receptors
  • Alzheimer's: low acetylcholine; anticholinergic drugs worsen confusion
C7

Upper vs. lower motor neuron

Upper motor neurons (brain and cord) control and restrain reflexes; damage causes spastic weakness and brisk reflexes. Lower motor neurons (anterior horn cells and peripheral nerves) connect to muscle; damage causes flaccid weakness, low reflexes, and atrophy.

UMN: spastic, hyperreflexia, + Babinski · LMN: flaccid, hyporeflexia, atrophy, fasciculations

Picture it: The manager vs. the worker: lose the manager and the worker acts without restraint; lose the worker and nothing gets done.

Where you'll see it
  • Stroke and cord injury above the lesion level: UMN signs
  • Guillain-Barré and peripheral nerve injury: LMN signs
  • ALS: both

Neurological assessment

A neuro check is only useful when it is compared with the one before it. The trend tells you whether the brain is getting better or worse.

Level of consciousness comes first

LOC is the most sensitive indicator of neurological change. Early changes are subtle: restlessness, irritability, new confusion, or needing a louder voice or a touch to stay awake. Describe what the client does ("opens eyes to voice, oriented to name only") rather than using vague labels like "lethargic."

Worked example · read it step by step

Ms. Danielle Okafor, 46, fell from a ladder 4 hours ago. At 1400 she opened her eyes spontaneously, was oriented, and obeyed commands (GCS 15). At 1600 she opens her eyes only when you speak, says she is "at the store," and localizes when you press her nail bed. Her right pupil is 5 mm and sluggish; the left is 3 mm and brisk. BP 152/70, HR 64.

  1. Score it. Eyes 3 + verbal 4 + motor 5 = GCS 12, down from 15.
  2. Look at the trend. A drop of 2 or more points is significant. LOC changed first.
  3. Add the pupils. A unilateral dilating, sluggish pupil suggests pressure on CN III on that side.
  4. Check the vitals. Widening pulse pressure and a falling heart rate are early Cushing's changes.
  5. Act. Notify the provider now (SBAR), elevate the head of the bed 30°, keep the head midline, and prepare for an emergency CT.

Cloudy CSF with high white cells, high protein, and low glucose suggests bacterial meningitis. Clear CSF with mildly high white cells and normal glucose suggests viral. Blood in CSF can mean a subarachnoid hemorrhage.

    Section 2 · Neurological Conditions

    Key concepts

    These principles explain the findings and the priorities across adult neurological conditions. They build on the Section 1 concepts.

    C1

    Time is brain

    During a stroke, millions of neurons die every minute that blood flow is blocked. The penumbra can be saved only if flow is restored quickly.

    Last known well → CT → thrombolytic within 4.5 h · thrombectomy up to 24 h

    Picture it: A house fire: every minute before the fire truck arrives, more rooms are lost.

    Where you'll see it
    • Note the time last known well
    • Door-to-CT and door-to-needle goals
    • TIA is a warning to act before the stroke
    C2

    Prevent the secondary injury

    After the first injury, swelling, hypoxia, hypotension, fever, and high CO₂ keep killing neurons for days. Nursing care can prevent this second wave.

    Secondary injury = hypoxia + hypotension + ↑ ICP + fever + seizures

    Picture it: After an earthquake, the aftershocks and fires can do more damage than the first shake.

    Where you'll see it
    • Swelling peaks 48–72 hours after injury or stroke
    • Keep SpO₂, MAP, CO₂, and temperature in range
    • Treat seizures quickly
    C3

    The seizure threshold

    Every brain balances excitation and inhibition. Anything that lowers the threshold (fever, low glucose, low sodium, alcohol withdrawal, sleep loss, missed meds) can tip it into a seizure.

    Excitation (glutamate) > inhibition (GABA) → seizure

    Picture it: A dam holding back water: lower the wall and the water spills over.

    Where you'll see it
    • Check glucose and sodium after a new seizure
    • Never stop antiseizure drugs abruptly
    • Benzodiazepines boost GABA to stop a seizure

    Myelin speeds the signal

    Myelin lets impulses jump quickly along an axon. When myelin is damaged, signals slow, scatter, or stop, so findings depend on which nerves are affected.

    Central demyelination → MS · Peripheral demyelination → Guillain-Barré

    Picture it: Stripped insulation on an electrical cord: the current leaks out and the lamp flickers.

    Where you'll see it
    • MS: vision changes, numbness, weakness that come and go
    • Heat slows conduction further in damaged nerves
    • GBS: absent reflexes and ascending weakness

    Weakness that worsens with use

    In myasthenia gravis, each nerve impulse has fewer working receptors to act on. Muscles start strong but tire with repeated use and recover with rest.

    Repeated use → fewer available ACh receptors → fatigue · Rest → recovery

    Picture it: A phone with a failing battery: fine after charging, dead by afternoon.

    Where you'll see it
    • Ptosis that worsens by evening
    • Schedule activity and meals for peak drug effect
    • Respiratory muscles can fail during infection or stress

    Airway first

    Many neuro conditions threaten the airway by lowering consciousness, weakening the swallow, or paralyzing the respiratory muscles. Airway and breathing come before everything else.

    GCS ≤ 8, weak cough or gag, or falling vital capacity → protect the airway

    Picture it: A guard who falls asleep at the gate: anything can get in.

    • Side-lying after a seizure

    Seizures and epilepsy

    A seizure is a burst of abnormal, excessive electrical activity in the brain. Epilepsy is a pattern of two or more unprovoked seizures. The cause may be fever, low glucose, low sodium, alcohol withdrawal, a tumor, a stroke, or no known reason.

    TypeWhat you see
    Focal awareTwitching or tingling in one area, odd smells or feelings; the client stays aware
    Focal impaired awarenessStaring, lip smacking, picking at clothes; confused afterward; may have an aura
    Generalized tonic-clonicLoss of consciousness, stiffening (tonic), then rhythmic jerking (clonic); possible cyanosis, incontinence, tongue biting; confused and sleepy afterward (postictal)
    AbsenceBrief staring spells, often in children; may happen many times a day
    Myoclonic, atonicSudden jerks; sudden loss of tone ("drop attacks")

    During a seizure

    1. Stay with the client and note the time. Call for help.
    2. Ease the client to the floor or lower the bed; protect the head; move objects away.
    3. Turn to the side when possible to let secretions drain.
    4. Loosen tight clothing. Do not restrain and put nothing in the mouth.
    5. Observe and document: onset, body parts involved, progression, duration, eyes, incontinence, postictal state.
    6. After: side-lying, suction and oxygen as needed, neuro checks, reorient, check glucose.
    Status epilepticus

    A seizure lasting 5 minutes or more, or repeated seizures without recovery between them, is an emergency. Expect IV lorazepam (or IM midazolam, rectal diazepam), then a loading dose of an antiseizure drug such as levetiracetam or fosphenytoin. Protect the airway.

    Precautions

    • Bed low, side rails padded per policy
    • Oxygen and suction at the bedside
    • IV access for high-risk clients
    • Take temperatures orally only if seizure risk is low

    • Take at the same time daily; never stop abruptly
    • Phenytoin: gingival hyperplasia (oral care), therapeutic level 10–20 mcg/mL; IV only with normal saline

    Parkinson's disease and multiple sclerosis

    Both are chronic and progressive. In Parkinson's, dopamine-making neurons die. In MS, the immune system strips myelin from the brain and cord.

    Parkinson's disease

    Findings (TRAP)

    • Tremor at rest ("pill rolling"), improves with movement
    • Rigidity (cogwheel)
    • Akinesia or bradykinesia; masklike face, soft voice
    • Postural instability; shuffling gait with a forward lean
    • Also: dysphagia, drooling, constipation, orthostatic hypotension, depression, dementia later

    Care

    • Carbidopa-levodopa on a strict schedule; protein can reduce absorption; watch for dyskinesias and "wearing off"
    • Other drugs: dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors, anticholinergics (avoid in older adults)
    • Fall prevention: wide stance, large steps, rocking to start, no throw rugs
    • Swallow precautions, small frequent meals, fiber and fluids
    • PT, OT, speech therapy; deep brain stimulation for some

    Multiple sclerosis

    • Autoimmune demyelination of the CNS, most often in women 20–40. Patterns: relapsing-remitting (most common) and progressive.
    • Findings depend on where plaques form: vision changes (optic neuritis, double vision), numbness and tingling, weakness, fatigue, spasticity, ataxia, bladder dysfunction, cognitive changes. Heat worsens symptoms (Uhthoff's phenomenon).
    • Diagnosis: MRI plaques, CSF oligoclonal bands.
    • Treatment: high-dose IV corticosteroids for relapses; disease-modifying therapies (interferons, glatiramer, monoclonal antibodies) to slow progression; baclofen for spasticity.
    • Care: energy conservation, avoid hot baths and overheating, infection prevention, bladder program, fall prevention, support coping.

    Myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, and ALS

    These three conditions all weaken muscles, but for different reasons. All three can stop a client from breathing, so respiratory assessment is the priority.

    Myasthenia gravis

    • Antibodies block acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction. Muscles weaken with use and improve with rest.
    • First signs are often in the eyes and face: ptosis, double vision, then trouble chewing, swallowing, and speaking; limb and respiratory weakness.

    • Acute autoimmune demyelination of peripheral nerves, often 1–3 weeks after a respiratory or GI infection (Campylobacter) or vaccination.

      Hydrocephalus and shunts

      Hydrocephalus is a buildup of CSF in the ventricles because flow is blocked or absorption fails. In an infant the skull expands; in an older child, pressure rises fast.

      • Causes: congenital narrowing (aqueductal stenosis), myelomeningocele (Chiari II malformation), bleeding in premature infants, meningitis, tumors.

      Neural tube defects

      The neural tube closes in the first month of pregnancy, often before a woman knows she is pregnant. When it fails to close, the spine, the cord, or the brain can be affected.

      TypeWhat it isEffects
      Spina bifida occultaThe vertebrae don't fully close; no sacOften no symptoms; dimple or tuft of hair over the lower back

      MyelomeningoceleSac containing meninges, CSF, and spinal cord and nervesParalysis and loss of sensation below the level; bowel and bladder dysfunction; most develop hydrocephalus
      AnencephalyMost of the brain and skull are missingNot compatible with life; comfort care

      Newborn with myelomeningocele

      1. Protect the sac. Cover with a sterile, moist, nonadherent dressing (sterile saline). Keep it from drying, tearing, or being soiled.
      2. Position prone (or side-lying) with hips slightly flexed; no diaper over the sac. Use a barrier below the sac to keep stool away.
      3. Watch for infection and leaking CSF; surgery is usually done within 24–72 hours.
      4. Measure head circumference daily and watch for signs of hydrocephalus.
      5. Latex precautions from birth; these children are at high risk for latex allergy.
      6. Assess movement and sensation of the legs, and urine and stool patterns.
      Long term and prevention

      Clean intermittent catheterization, bowel programs, braces or wheelchairs, skin checks (no sensation below the lesion), and school and family support. Folic acid 400 mcg daily for all who could become pregnant lowers the risk of neural tube defects.

      Infantile spasms

      Clusters of sudden flexing of the body ("jackknife") in an infant, often on waking, are an emergency referral. They can be linked to developmental regression.

      Duchenne muscular dystrophy

      Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is an inherited, progressive muscle disease. A missing protein (dystrophin) lets muscle fibers break down and be replaced by fat and scar tissue.

      • Inheritance: X-linked recessive, so it affects almost only boys. Mothers are carriers.

      • Gowers' sign: to stand up from the floor, the child "walks" his hands up his legs because the hip and thigh muscles are weak.

      • Labs: very high creatine kinase (CK); diagnosis confirmed by genetic testing.
      • Progression: wheelchair by about age 12; scoliosis and contractures; respiratory muscle weakness and cardiomyopathy in the teens and twenties.

      Care

      Keep the child active and safe

      • Corticosteroids slow loss of strength (watch growth, weight, bones, mood)
      • Stretching, night splints, PT to delay contractures
      • Prevent obesity, which makes moving harder
      • Falls and fracture prevention

      Protect breathing and the heart

      • Pulmonary function tests; incentive spirometry, cough assist
      • Vaccines; prompt treatment of respiratory infections
      • Noninvasive ventilation as needed
      • Regular cardiac evaluation
      • Genetic counseling; family and school support

        These principles help you sort out cognitive changes and plan safe care.

        C1

        A sudden change is a medical problem

        Any sudden change in thinking or attention is delirium until proven otherwise. It signals an underlying medical problem that needs to be found and treated.

        New confusion over hours to days → look for infection, drugs, metabolic, oxygen, pain, retention

        Picture it: A smoke alarm going off: you look for the fire, you don't just turn off the alarm.

        Where you'll see it

        • Post-op delirium
        • Delirium on top of dementia
        C2

        Attention separates delirium from dementia

        Delirium is a disorder of attention. Clients with early dementia can still focus on a conversation even when they can't remember it.

        Inattention + acute onset = delirium (CAM)

        Picture it: A radio with static (delirium) vs. a radio playing clearly but forgetting the song it just played (dementia).

        • Months backward, spelling WORLD backward

          Sympathetic fibers leave the cord at T1–L2, so an injury at T6 or above cuts the brain off from them. The vagus nerve (parasympathetic) still works, so the heart slows and vessels dilate.

          • Neurogenic shock
          • Orthostatic hypotension
          • Poikilothermia

          Picture it: A thermostat whose wire to the control panel is cut: the furnace runs full blast.

          • Autonomic dysreflexia
          • Spastic muscles and a reflex bladder

          Section 5 · Spinal Cord Conditions

          Spinal cord injury

          Most spinal cord injuries come from motor vehicle crashes, falls, violence, and sports such as diving. The first hit damages the cord; bleeding, swelling, and low blood flow over the next hours make it worse.

          TypeWhat is lost
          CompleteAll motor and sensory function below the level, including the lowest sacral segments
          Central cord syndromeArms weaker than legs; often older adults with hyperextension injuries
          Anterior cord syndromeMotor function, pain, and temperature below the level; touch and position sense kept
          Brown-Séquard syndromeHalf the cord (often a stab wound): motor and position loss on the same side, pain and temperature loss on the opposite side
          Cauda equina syndromeNerve roots below L1: leg weakness, saddle numbness, bowel and bladder dysfunction; a surgical emergency

          Emergency and acute care

          1. Immobilize the spine (cervical collar, log roll, firm surface) for any trauma with possible spinal injury.
          2. Airway with spinal precautions: jaw thrust, not head tilt. Watch for rising injuries that weaken the diaphragm.
          3. Breathing: monitor vital capacity, SpO₂, ABGs; weak cough needs assisted coughing ("quad cough") and suctioning.
          4. Circulation: treat hypotension; many protocols keep MAP 85–90 mm Hg for the first week to perfuse the cord.
          5. Neuro checks of motor strength and sensation in every dermatome; report any upward change.
          6. Stabilization: surgery (decompression and fusion), skeletal traction, or a halo vest.
          7. Prevent complications: DVT prophylaxis, indwelling catheter at first, NG tube for paralytic ileus, stress ulcer prophylaxis, pressure injury prevention.
          Halo vest care

          Check pin sites for infection and clean per protocol; keep the wrench taped to the vest for emergency removal of the front panel; never use the frame to lift or turn the client; check skin under the vest.

          Autonomic dysreflexia

          Autonomic dysreflexia is a life-threatening emergency in clients with injuries at T6 or above, usually after spinal shock is over. A stimulus below the injury sets off a massive sympathetic reflex that the brain can't shut down.

          Triggers

          • Full bladder (most common): kinked catheter, missed catheterization, UTI
          • Bowel: constipation, impaction
          • Skin: pressure injury, tight clothing, ingrown toenail, burn
          • Menstrual cramps, labor, sexual activity, procedures

          Findings

          • Sudden severe hypertension (can exceed 200 systolic)
          • Pounding headache
          • Bradycardia
          • Flushing and sweating above the injury; pale, cool skin with goosebumps below
          • Nasal stuffiness, blurred vision, anxiety

          What to do, in order

          1. Sit the client upright and lower the legs to pool blood away from the brain.
          2. Loosen tight clothing, binders, and stockings.
          3. Check the bladder: look for kinks in the catheter; irrigate or replace it; if no catheter, catheterize.
          4. Check the bowel for impaction using a numbing (lidocaine) gel first.
          5. Check the skin for pressure, wrinkles, or injury.
          6. Monitor BP every 2–5 minutes. If it stays high after removing the cause, expect a fast-acting antihypertensive (nifedipine or nitroglycerin per protocol).
          7. Notify the provider; document; teach the client and family to recognize and treat it.

            Key concepts

            These principles explain musculoskeletal findings and the complications nurses work to prevent.

            C1

            Use it or lose it

            Bone, muscle, and joints need movement and load to stay healthy. Without them, muscles weaken, joints stiffen, and bone loses calcium within days.

            Immobility → atrophy, contractures, bone loss, ↑ blood calcium

            Picture it: A bicycle left out all winter: the chain rusts stiff from not being used.

            Where you'll see it
            • Hazards of immobility
            • Early ambulation after surgery
            • Weight-bearing exercise for osteoporosis
            C2

            Bone remodels under load

            Bone is constantly broken down by osteoclasts and rebuilt by osteoblasts. Load, calcium, vitamin D, and sex hormones tip the balance toward building.

            Load + calcium + vitamin D + hormones → building > breakdown

            Picture it: A road crew that repaves where the traffic is heaviest.

            Where you'll see it
            • Osteoporosis after menopause
            • Fracture healing stages
            • Bisphosphonates slow osteoclasts
            C3

            Pressure in a closed space

            Muscles sit in compartments wrapped by fascia that doesn't stretch. Swelling or a tight cast raises pressure until it squeezes the capillaries and nerves shut.

            ↑ volume in a fixed fascial space → ↑ pressure → ↓ capillary flow → ischemia

            Picture it: Stuffing more and more into a sealed bag until it presses on everything inside.

            Where you'll see it
            • Compartment syndrome
            • Tight casts and dressings
            • Elevate to heart level, not above
            C4

            Check beyond the injury

            Circulation, motion, and sensation beyond an injury, cast, or surgery tell you whether blood and nerve supply are intact. Compare with the other side and with the last check.

            CMS: color, warmth, cap refill, pulse · movement · sensation

            Picture it: Checking the lights downstream after a repair on the power line.

            Where you'll see it
            • Casts and traction
            • After orthopedic surgery
            • Early sign of compartment syndrome: pain and paresthesia
            C5

            Stillness invites clots

            Clots form with slow flow, vessel injury, and a clotting tendency (Virchow's triad). Orthopedic surgery and immobility create all three.

            Stasis + vessel injury + hypercoagulability → DVT → PE

            Picture it: A still pond grows algae; a flowing stream doesn't.

            Where you'll see it
            • DVT after hip and knee surgery
            • Anticoagulants and SCDs

            Musculoskeletal structure and assessment

            Bone is living tissue that is constantly broken down and rebuilt. Joints, ligaments, tendons, and muscles let it move.

            Osteoporosis, osteoarthritis, and gout

            Three common chronic conditions: one weakens bone, one wears out cartilage, and one deposits crystals in joints.

            Osteoporosis

            • Bone breakdown outpaces building, so bone becomes porous and fragile. Risk factors: female, after menopause, older age, small frame, white or Asian ancestry, smoking, alcohol, inactivity, low calcium and vitamin D, long-term corticosteroids.
            • Often silent until a fracture: hip, wrist, or vertebral compression fractures with loss of height and kyphosis.
            • Diagnosis: DEXA scan (T-score −2.5 or lower).
            • Prevention and treatment: calcium (1,000–1,200 mg/day) and vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, fall prevention, no smoking. Medications: bisphosphonates such as alendronate (take on an empty stomach with a full glass of water, stay upright 30 minutes), denosumab, raloxifene.

            Osteoarthritis

            • Wear and tear of cartilage in weight-bearing joints (knees, hips, spine, hands)
            • Pain worse with activity and late in the day; stiffness under 30 minutes after rest; crepitus; Heberden's nodes
            • Weight loss, exercise, heat, acetaminophen or NSAIDs, injections; joint replacement

            Gout

            • Uric acid crystals deposit in joints, often the big toe
            • Sudden, severe pain, redness, swelling, often at night
            • Acute: NSAIDs, colchicine, corticosteroids, rest, ice
            • Prevention: allopurinol; fluids 2–3 L/day; limit alcohol, red meat, organ meats, shellfish, and high-fructose drinks
            Rheumatoid arthritis is different

            RA is autoimmune and systemic: symmetric small-joint swelling, morning stiffness over an hour, fatigue, and fever. You'll study it with immunity.

              Key concepts

              These principles explain how sight and hearing are lost and what that means for care.

              C1

              Light needs a clear path

              Light must pass through a clear cornea, lens, and vitreous and land on a healthy retina. Anything that clouds the path or damages the retina blurs or blocks vision.

              Cornea → aqueous → lens → vitreous → retina → optic nerve → occipital lobe

              Picture it: A camera: a smudged lens blurs the picture; a damaged sensor leaves part of it blank.

              Where you'll see it
              • Cataract clouds the lens
              • Macular degeneration damages the center of the retina
              • Retinal detachment pulls the 'sensor' away
              C2

              Pressure damages the optic nerve

              Aqueous humor is made and drained continuously. If drainage falls behind, intraocular pressure rises and slowly or suddenly damages the optic nerve. Lost nerve fibers don't come back.

              Production > drainage → ↑ IOP → optic nerve damage → peripheral vision loss

              Picture it: A sink with a slow drain and the tap left running.

              Where you'll see it
              • Open-angle glaucoma: slow and painless
              • Angle-closure: sudden and painful
              • After eye surgery: avoid actions that raise pressure
              C3

              Hearing is a two-part system

              Sound must be conducted through the outer and middle ear, then sensed by the cochlea and nerve. Where the problem is decides the type of loss and the treatment.

              Conductive: outer or middle ear · Sensorineural: cochlea or CN VIII

              Picture it: A phone call: a bad line (conductive) vs. a broken speaker (sensorineural).

              Where you'll see it
              • Cerumen and otitis media are conductive
              • Presbycusis and ototoxicity are sensorineural
              • Weber and Rinne tests tell them apart
              C4

              Sudden change is an emergency

              Gradual vision or hearing loss needs follow-up. Sudden loss, sudden eye pain, or flashes and a curtain over vision need emergency care to save the sense.

              Sudden: retinal detachment, angle-closure glaucoma, chemical injury, sudden hearing loss

              Picture it: A slow leak in a tire can wait a day; a blowout can't.

              Where you'll see it
              • Flashes and floaters
              • Severe eye pain with nausea
              • Chemical splash: irrigate first
              C5

              Sensory loss isolates and endangers

              Clients who can't see or hear well miss cues, fall, misunderstand instructions, withdraw socially, and can appear confused. Restoring input and adapting communication protects safety and cognition.

              Sensory loss → falls, isolation, depression, misread as confusion or dementia

              Picture it: Walking through a dark house: you bump into things you'd normally step around.

              Where you'll see it
              • Glasses and hearing aids prevent delirium
              • Fall risk with vision loss and vertigo

              Light passes through the cornea, aqueous humor, lens, and vitreous to reach the retina. The retina turns light into signals that travel along the optic nerve (cranial nerve II) to the occipital lobe.

              CorneaClear front window; bends light; very sensitive (corneal reflex, CN V)

              Eye medications, surgery, and injuries

              Eye drops work locally but can act on the whole body. After eye surgery, the goal is to protect the eye and keep pressure from rising.

              Eye drop technique

              1. Wash hands; tilt the head back and look up.
              2. Pull the lower lid down to form a pocket and place the drop there, not on the cornea; don't touch the tip to the eye.
              3. Close the eye gently and press the inner corner (punctal occlusion) for 1–2 minutes to reduce absorption into the body.
              4. Wait at least 5 minutes between different drops.

              The ear and hearing

              Hearing takes two steps: sound waves must be conducted through the outer and middle ear, then sensed by the cochlea and carried by the acoustic nerve (cranial nerve VIII). The inner ear also controls balance.

              PartStructuresJob
              Outer earAuricle, ear canalCollects sound; cerumen protects the canal
              Middle earTympanic membrane, three ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes), eustachian tubeConducts and amplifies sound; the eustachian tube equalizes pressure
              Inner earCochlea; semicircular canals and vestibuleCochlea: turns vibration into nerve signals · Vestibular system: balance

              Assessment

              • Otoscope: in adults pull the auricle up and back; in children under 3, down and back. A normal eardrum is pearly gray with a cone of light.
              • Whisper test: whisper words from about 2 feet while the client covers the other ear.
              • Weber test: tuning fork on top of the head. Normal: heard equally. Conductive loss: louder in the affected ear. Sensorineural loss: louder in the good ear.
              • Rinne test: compare air conduction (beside the ear) with bone conduction (on the mastoid). Normal: air longer than bone. Conductive loss: bone longer than or equal to air.
              • Audiometry measures hearing across pitches.
              • Balance: Romberg test; ask about vertigo (a spinning sensation).

              Sensory loss, deprivation, and overload

              Too little meaningful stimulation and too much meaningless stimulation both disturb thinking. Clients with vision or hearing loss are at risk for both, and for isolation, depression, falls, and delirium.

              Sensory deprivation

              • Isolation, long stays, blindfolding, sensory loss, monotony
              • Boredom, confusion, hallucinations, depression
              • Provide meaningful stimulation: conversation, clocks, radio, windows, visitors, glasses and hearing aids

              Sensory overload

              • ICU noise, alarms, lights, pain, constant interruptions
              • Anxiety, agitation, confusion, poor sleep
              • Reduce noise and lights at night, cluster care, explain sounds, private time

              Communicating with a client who has a hearing loss

              • Get attention first; face the client in good light; don't cover your mouth.
              • Speak clearly at a normal or slightly louder volume and a lower pitch. Don't shout; it distorts speech.
              • Rephrase rather than repeat; reduce background noise; use writing, pictures, or an interpreter for clients who use sign language.
              • Make sure hearing aids are in, clean, and working.

              Caring for a client with vision loss

              • Announce yourself when you enter and tell the client when you leave.
              • Orient to the room; keep furniture and items in the same place; describe where things are using a clock face ("your coffee is at 3 o'clock").
              • Offer your arm when walking (the client holds your elbow and walks half a step behind).
              • Good lighting without glare, high-contrast and large-print materials, magnifiers, talking devices.
              The other senses

              Taste and smell decline with age, medications, and smoking: watch for poor appetite, oversalting, and missing spoiled food or gas leaks (smoke and gas detectors). Reduced touch and temperature sensation (diabetes, stroke, SCI) means checking bath water with a thermometer and inspecting the feet daily.
