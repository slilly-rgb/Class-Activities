    Part 1 · Apply it

    Preload, afterload, or contractility?

    New scenarios. Which determinant changed, and which way? Talk with your table first.

    Severe dehydration from vomiting

    Reveal
    Preload ↓

    Less volume returning, less stretch. The balloon is barely filled.

    Nitroglycerin given for chest pain

    Reveal
    Preload ↓

    It dilates veins, so they hold more blood and less returns to the heart. That eases the heart’s workload.

    20 years of untreated hypertension

    Reveal
    Afterload ↑

    A constant thumb over the hose. The left ventricle thickens over time, like a muscle that lifts weights every day.

    Septic shock with widespread vasodilation

    Reveal
    Afterload ↓

    Wide-open vessels offer little resistance. Blood also pools in dilated veins, so preload drops too.

    A beta blocker is started

    Reveal
    Contractility ↓

    A gentler squeeze of the bottle, and a slower heart rate.

    Epinephrine during a code

    Reveal
    Contractility ↑

    A harder squeeze and a faster rate. It also constricts vessels, which raises afterload.

    Six scenarios students did not see at home. Give tables 20–30 seconds each, then reveal. Push for the “why” using the analogies: balloon (preload), thumb over the hose (afterload), ketchup bottle (contractility). Septic shock and epinephrine change more than one determinant.

    Part 1 · Unfolding case · NCSBN clinical judgment

    Mr. Duncan, 58, chest pressure

    HR112
    BP98/62
    SpO₂94%
    RR22

    Arrived in the ED with chest pressure for one hour. Skin is pale, cool, and clammy. History of smoking and high LDL. Fictional case.

    1 · Recognize cues

    Which findings matter most?

    Reveal

    Chest pressure, HR 112, soft BP, cool clammy skin, and modifiable risk factors (smoking, LDL).

    2 · Analyze cues

    What is his MAP, and what is the heart doing?

    Reveal

    MAP ≈ (98 + 2 × 62) ÷ 3 = 74, holding for now. If muscle is injured, contractility and SV fall, and HR rises to keep CO = SV × HR. Cool skin means the body is shunting blood to the core.

    3 · Prioritize hypotheses

    What is the most urgent problem?

    Reveal

    Myocardial ischemia or injury reducing his cardiac output.

    4 · Generate solutions

    Which tests answer the question fastest?

    Reveal

    A 12-lead ECG for the rhythm and ST changes, and troponin to check for muscle injury. His troponin comes back elevated, and a cardiac cath is planned.

    5 · Take action

    After the cath he says, “I’ll mow the lawn tomorrow.” Your response?

    Reveal

    Correct it: limit activity for several days, no heavy lifting, keep the dressing on at least the first day, and watch the site for signs of infection.

    6 · Evaluate outcomes

    What tells you he’s improving?

    Reveal

    Chest pressure relieved, HR trending down, MAP holding at 60–70 or above, warm skin, and he can teach back his home care.

    Fictional unfolding case that pulls together cardiac output, MAP, assessment, diagnostics, post-cath teaching, and the six-step model. Give tables a minute per step, have one table report out, then reveal.

    Part 1 · NCLEX-style check

    Check yourself

    Click an answer, or advance to reveal each one.

    1. A client’s heart rate is 120 beats/min and stroke volume is 40 mL. What is the cardiac output?

    120 × 40 = 4,800 mL/min. Just under the normal 5–6 L/min, and only because the fast heart rate is compensating for a low stroke volume.

    2. Two hours after a femoral cardiac catheterization, which finding requires the nurse’s immediate action?

    A weaker pulse and a cool foot below the insertion site suggest blood flow to the leg is blocked. That needs action now.

    3. Which lab result is the most specific sign of myocardial injury?

    Troponin is specific to heart muscle. Total CK also rises with skeletal muscle injury, and lipids describe risk, not injury.

    Answers: 1 B, 2 B, 3 A. Have students vote (hands or Vevox) before each reveal.

    Part 2 · Count the boxes

    Measure it

    One small box = 0.04 s. One large box = 0.20 s. Count, then reveal.

    Strip A · PR interval

    How long is the PR interval? Normal?

    Reveal

    0.28s · 7 small boxes

    Longer than 0.20 s: the AV node is slow to conduct (first-degree AV block).

    Strip B · QRS width

    How wide is the QRS? Normal?

    Reveal

    0.14s · 3.5 small boxes

    Wider than 0.12 s: a slow or abnormal route through the ventricles, such as a bundle branch block.

    Strip C · Heart rate

    What is the heart rate?

    Reveal

    100beats/min

    The R waves are 3 large boxes apart: 300 ÷ 3 = 100.

    Have students count boxes at their seats before each reveal. Revealing a card also draws the caliper on its strip. A: PR 0.28 s, prolonged (first-degree AV block). B: QRS 0.14 s, wide. C: R waves 3 large boxes apart, rate 100.

    Part 2 · Bedside

    What would the monitor show?

    Predict the ECG change and explain why, using the electrophysiology.

    Serum potassium is 2.9 mEq/L.

    Reveal
    Flattened T waves, possible U waves

    Repolarization (phase 3) depends on K⁺ leaving the cell, so potassium problems show up in the T wave.

    The SA node stops firing. The monitor shows a regular rhythm at 48 with no P waves and narrow QRS complexes.

    Reveal
    The AV junction took over

    Its backup rate is 40–60/min. No P waves because the atria aren’t starting the beat; a narrow QRS because the impulse still travels the normal route through the ventricles.

    Crushing chest pain. The ST segment sits well above the TP baseline.

    Reveal
    Possible myocardial injury

    Injured muscle can’t hold the same charge during the plateau, so the ST segment shifts. ST elevation with chest pain is an emergency.

    Heart rate has been 150 for hours and the patient now reports chest pain.

    Reveal
    Shrinking TP segment

    Fast rates cut diastole first. Less filling time lowers stroke volume, and the coronary arteries, which fill in diastole, deliver less oxygen to the heart muscle.

    Four bedside scenarios that connect the electrophysiology to patients. Have tables predict the ECG change and the reason before each reveal.

    Part 2 · NCLEX-style check

    Check yourself

    Click an answer, or advance to reveal each one.

    4. During phase 2 of the ventricular action potential, which ion movement keeps the cell depolarized and powers contraction?

    The plateau holds because Ca²⁺ flows in while K⁺ flows out. The calcium triggers contraction. On the ECG this is the ST segment.

    5. The R waves on a regular strip are 2 large boxes apart. What is the heart rate?

    300 ÷ 2 = 150 beats/min.

    6. The monitor shows a regular rhythm at 34/min, wide QRS complexes, and no P waves. Where is the pacemaker most likely located?

    A rate of 20–40 and a wide QRS point to a ventricular pacemaker. The impulse starts low and takes a slow route through the muscle.

    Answers: 4 B, 5 C, 6 C. Have students vote before each reveal.
